\section{P2P: ML-KEM + ML-DSA peer handshake}
\label{sec:p2p-pq}

\subsection{Why the P2P layer matters}

A chain whose consensus, transaction authorisation, and contract permits are
all post-quantum is still classically vulnerable if peers connect over a
classical Diffie-Hellman handshake. A Shor-capable adversary on the wire
breaks the handshake, recovers the session key, and replays / forges peer
messages with the same confidentiality guarantees as the original. The peer
authentication primitive is the gating point.

Classical Lux peers used ed25519-static-keys + X25519-ephemeral-keys for the
Noise-XK handshake. Both are classical: Shor breaks them. Strict-PQ replaces
both: ML-KEM for the key exchange, ML-DSA for the static identity. The
reference impl is \texttt{luxfi/node@dc906d281b}.

\subsection{Construction}

The handshake is a Noise-XK variant where:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,nosep]
  \item The static identity key is ML-DSA-65 (matching the chain's
    \texttt{IdentitySchemeID}).
  \item The ephemeral key encapsulation is ML-KEM-768 by default, ML-KEM-1024
    for high-value links (validator-to-validator, bridge-to-bridge).
  \item The session key derivation hashes through five cSHAKE customizations
    that domain-separate the handshake stages.
\end{itemize}

The five cSHAKE customizations are:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{P2P handshake cSHAKE customizations. Each stage of the handshake
uses an independent customization to prevent cross-stage replay.}
\label{tab:p2p-cust}
\begin{tabular}{lp{7cm}}
\toprule
Customization & Stage \\
\midrule
\texttt{LUX\_P2P\_HANDSHAKE\_INIT\_V1}   & initial KEM ciphertext binding \\
\texttt{LUX\_P2P\_HANDSHAKE\_RESP\_V1}   & responder KEM commitment binding \\
\texttt{LUX\_P2P\_HANDSHAKE\_AUTH\_V1}   & static-key authentication binding \\
\texttt{LUX\_P2P\_HANDSHAKE\_KEY\_V1}    & session key derivation \\
\texttt{LUX\_P2P\_HANDSHAKE\_REKEY\_V1}  & rekey rotation derivation \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Each cSHAKE call binds the customization string plus the relevant
transcript bytes. The handshake transcript binds: protocol version, both
parties' ML-DSA-65 identity-pubkey hashes, ML-KEM-768 / 1024 ciphertext,
the responder's confirmation byte, and the negotiated session parameters.

\subsection{Why ML-KEM-768 by default and ML-KEM-1024 for high-value links}

ML-KEM-768 is NIST Level 3 (192-bit). ML-KEM-1024 is NIST Level 5
(256-bit). The cost ratio is:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{ML-KEM cost comparison. Numbers from FIPS 203 §8.2 reference impl
on commodity x86-64.}
\label{tab:mlkem-cost}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Variant & Pubkey & Ciphertext & Keygen + Encaps + Decaps (\texttt{$\mu$s}) \\
\midrule
ML-KEM-512   & 800 B    & 768 B    & 25 \\
ML-KEM-768   & 1{,}184 B & 1{,}088 B & 35 \\
ML-KEM-1024  & 1{,}568 B & 1{,}568 B & 55 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

For peer-to-peer links among $\sim 10^4$ peers handshaking $\sim 10^2$ times
per peer per hour, the bandwidth difference between ML-KEM-768 and
ML-KEM-1024 is $\sim 2$ MB / hour --- negligible. For validator-to-validator
links handshaking many times per minute and carrying high-value finality
traffic, the 256-bit security floor is worth the cost. The strict-PQ
profile pins per-link KEM choice based on the link's classification
(validator, bridge, regular peer); the default is ML-KEM-768.

\subsection{Handshake flow}

The Noise-XK variant:

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Strict-PQ peer handshake (Noise-XK over ML-KEM + ML-DSA-65)}
\label{alg:p2p-handshake}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initiator $I$ with static ML-DSA-65 key $(\textsc{sk}_I, \textsc{pk}_I)$
\Require Responder $R$ with static ML-DSA-65 key $(\textsc{sk}_R, \textsc{pk}_R)$
\Require Initiator knows $\textsc{pk}_R$ a priori (Noise-XK property)
\State $I$ samples ephemeral ML-KEM keypair $(\textsc{kpk}_I, \textsc{ksk}_I)$
\State $I$ sends $\langle \textsc{kpk}_I, \textsc{tag}_1 \rangle$ where $\textsc{tag}_1 = \textsc{cSHAKE256}(\texttt{INIT}, \textsc{kpk}_I)$
\State $R$ encapsulates against $\textsc{kpk}_I$, deriving shared secret $s_1$
\State $R$ sends $\langle \textsc{ct}_R, \textsc{aead}_{s_1}(\textsc{pk}_R), \textsc{tag}_2 \rangle$
  where $\textsc{tag}_2 = \textsc{cSHAKE256}(\texttt{RESP}, \textsc{ct}_R, \textsc{enc}(\textsc{pk}_R))$
\State $I$ decapsulates, recovers $s_1$, AEAD-decrypts $\textsc{pk}_R'$, confirms $\textsc{pk}_R' = \textsc{pk}_R$
\State $I$ samples second ephemeral ML-KEM keypair, encapsulates against $\textsc{pk}_R$
  to derive $s_2$
\State $I$ sends $\langle \textsc{ct}_I, \textsc{aead}_{s_2}(\textsc{pk}_I, \textsc{sig}_I), \textsc{tag}_3 \rangle$
  where $\textsc{sig}_I = \textsc{ML-DSA-65.Sign}_{\textsc{sk}_I}(\textsc{cSHAKE256}(\texttt{AUTH}, \ldots))$
\State $R$ verifies $\textsc{sig}_I$, derives session key $K = \textsc{cSHAKE256}(\texttt{KEY}, s_1, s_2)$
\State Both parties begin AEAD-protected data exchange under $K$
\State Rekey at message-count or time-bound thresholds using $\texttt{REKEY}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Two ML-KEM encapsulations form the session key. The first binds the
responder's identity; the second binds the initiator's identity. ML-DSA-65
authenticates the initiator over the bound transcript. The responder is
authenticated by Noise-XK's a-priori-pubkey property (the initiator confirms
$\textsc{pk}_R$ during step 5).

\subsection{Five customizations, not one}

Why five customization strings? The naive design uses a single
\texttt{LUX\_P2P\_V1} customization for every cSHAKE call. The five-cust
design is the audit-driven minimum that closes:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Cross-stage replay} (between INIT, RESP, AUTH, KEY, REKEY)
    is closed by per-stage customizations.
  \item \textbf{Initiator-impersonating-responder} attacks (where an
    attacker sends an INIT-tagged message to a peer expecting RESP) are
    closed by distinct INIT and RESP customizations.
  \item \textbf{Rekey-as-fresh-session} attacks (where a rekeyed session is
    misinterpreted as a fresh session) are closed by the dedicated REKEY
    customization.
\end{itemize}

The five-cust design is the Noise-protocol-framework minimum applied
verbatim to cSHAKE256.

\subsection{Static identity in Z-Chain}

Peer static ML-DSA-65 keys are registered in the Z-Chain identity rollup
under \texttt{OpRegisterKey} alongside transaction-signing keys. The
\texttt{AccountID} of a peer is the chain's canonical peer identifier (which
HIP-0077 §3 names \texttt{TypedNodeID} --- see
Section~\ref{sec:cpx-coverage}). The handshake's static-pubkey verification
walks back to the Z-Chain identity rollup; the peer is admitted iff its
pubkey is on the active set at the current epoch.

This integrates the P2P identity primitive with the chain identity primitive:
a peer that loses validator status (revoked, rotated) is automatically
dropped from the peer set at the next epoch transition. The current-epoch
\texttt{IdentityRoot} is the source of truth.

\subsection{No classical fallback}

The handshake refuses classical primitives. There is no Curve25519
fallback, no ed25519 fallback, no AES-GCM-with-classical-KEM mode. A peer
that advertises only classical primitives is refused at the version
negotiation step (the protocol version byte is checked before any KEM /
DSA work, so the refusal is cheap).

Classical-compat peers (running the permissive profile) reach the strict-PQ
chain only through a translation gateway --- they cannot directly join the
peer mesh.
