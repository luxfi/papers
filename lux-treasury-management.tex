\begin{abstract}
We present a formal framework for decentralized autonomous organization (DAO) treasury management, addressing fund allocation, spending proposals, and portfolio diversification. Our mechanism combines quadratic funding for public goods with conviction voting for ongoing grants, achieving both allocative efficiency and Sybil resistance. We introduce a diversification protocol based on modern portfolio theory adapted for crypto-native treasuries, with formal bounds on drawdown risk. The spending proposal system employs a multi-stage approval process with retroactive funding elements. We prove that the combined mechanism satisfies budget balance, individual rationality, and approximate incentive compatibility. Simulation results demonstrate robustness across market conditions and governance participation levels.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Decentralized autonomous organizations accumulate significant treasuries through protocol revenues, token reserves, and ecosystem funds. Effective treasury management requires solving three interconnected problems:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \textbf{Fund Allocation}: How should limited resources be distributed among competing priorities?
    \item \textbf{Spending Proposals}: What process governs individual spending decisions?
    \item \textbf{Diversification}: How should treasury assets be managed to ensure long-term sustainability?
\end{enumerate}

The Lux Network treasury serves as a public good funding mechanism for ecosystem development. This paper presents a comprehensive framework balancing community preferences, economic efficiency, and risk management.

\subsection{Challenges}

DAO treasury management faces unique challenges:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Plutocracy}: Token-weighted voting favors large holders
    \item \textbf{Voter Apathy}: Low participation undermines legitimacy
    \item \textbf{Information Asymmetry}: Voters lack expertise to evaluate proposals
    \item \textbf{Coordination Failure}: Collective action problems in public goods funding
    \item \textbf{Treasury Attacks}: Malicious proposals extracting value
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Related Work}

Quadratic funding was introduced by Buterin, Hitzig, and Weyl \cite{buterin2018qf} for public goods allocation. Conviction voting provides continuous preference aggregation \cite{conviction2019}. MolochDAO pioneered ragequit mechanisms for treasury protection \cite{moloch2019}. Yearn's yDAO and Compound's grants program established operational patterns \cite{yearn2021, compound2021}.

Portfolio theory for crypto treasuries extends classical mean-variance optimization \cite{markowitz1952} with considerations for volatility, correlation, and liquidity unique to digital assets \cite{liu2021crypto}.

\subsection{Contributions}

This paper contributes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Integrated framework combining quadratic funding and conviction voting
    \item Multi-stage proposal system with retroactive elements
    \item Crypto-native portfolio diversification with risk bounds
    \item Formal analysis of mechanism properties
    \item Implementation design for the Lux treasury
\end{itemize}

\section{System Model}

\subsection{Treasury State}

\begin{definition}[Treasury]
The treasury $\mathcal{T} = (A, L, G)$ where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $A = \{(a_1, q_1), \ldots, (a_k, q_k)\}$ is the asset portfolio (asset type, quantity)
    \item $L$ is committed liabilities (approved but unexecuted spending)
    \item $G \subseteq \mathcal{P}$ is the set of active grants
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

Treasury value at time $t$:
\begin{equation}
    V(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} q_i \cdot p_i(t) - L
\end{equation}
where $p_i(t)$ is the price of asset $a_i$ at time $t$.

\subsection{Participants}

\begin{definition}[Governance Participants]
The set $\mathcal{G} = \{g_1, \ldots, g_n\}$ where each $g_i$ holds governance tokens $\tau_i$ and can:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Submit proposals
    \item Vote on proposals
    \item Delegate voting power
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\subsection{Proposal Types}

\begin{definition}[Spending Proposal]
A spending proposal $p = (\text{recipient}, \text{amount}, \text{asset}, \text{purpose}, \text{milestones})$ requests treasury disbursement.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Investment Proposal]
An investment proposal $p = (\text{from}, \text{to}, \text{amount}, \text{rationale})$ requests portfolio rebalancing.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Parameter Proposal]
A parameter proposal $p = (\text{param}, \text{old\_value}, \text{new\_value})$ modifies treasury parameters.
\end{definition}

\section{Fund Allocation Mechanism}

\subsection{Quadratic Funding for Public Goods}

For public goods with positive externalities, we employ quadratic funding:

\begin{definition}[Quadratic Funding]
Given projects $\{p_1, \ldots, p_m\}$ and contributions $c_{ij}$ from participant $g_i$ to project $p_j$:
\begin{equation}
    F_j = \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sqrt{c_{ij}} \right)^2
\end{equation}
The matching pool $M$ is distributed proportionally:
\begin{equation}
    M_j = M \cdot \frac{F_j - \sum_i c_{ij}}{\sum_k (F_k - \sum_i c_{ik})}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Optimality of Quadratic Funding]
Quadratic funding achieves the utilitarian optimum for public goods when:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Participants have quasilinear utility
    \item Projects are independent
    \item No collusion occurs
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The social welfare function is:
\begin{equation}
    W = \sum_j V_j(F_j) - \sum_i \sum_j c_{ij}
\end{equation}
where $V_j$ is the aggregate value of project $j$.

First-order condition for participant $i$:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial V_j}{\partial F_j} \cdot \frac{\partial F_j}{\partial c_{ij}} = 1
\end{equation}

With $\frac{\partial F_j}{\partial c_{ij}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{ij}}} \cdot \sum_k \sqrt{c_{kj}}$, the equilibrium satisfies:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial V_j}{\partial F_j} = \frac{\sqrt{c_{ij}}}{\sum_k \sqrt{c_{kj}}}
\end{equation}

Aggregating across participants yields the Samuelson condition for public goods optimality.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Sybil Resistance}

Quadratic funding is vulnerable to Sybil attacks:

\begin{definition}[Sybil Attack]
A Sybil attack occurs when participant splits identity into $k$ accounts, each contributing $c/k$, yielding:
\begin{equation}
    k \cdot \sqrt{c/k} = \sqrt{kc} > \sqrt{c}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Identity Verification]
We require identity verification $\text{ID}: \mathcal{G} \to \{0, 1\}$ where only verified participants ($\text{ID}(g_i) = 1$) can contribute to quadratic funding rounds.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Bounded Sybil Gain]
With identity verification cost $\kappa$ per identity, the maximum gain from Sybil attack is bounded:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Gain} \leq \sqrt{c \cdot M / C} - \sqrt{c \cdot M / C} - k \cdot \kappa
\end{equation}
where $C$ is total contributions. Attack unprofitable when $\kappa > \sqrt{c \cdot M / C} / k$.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Conviction Voting for Ongoing Grants}

For continuous funding decisions, we employ conviction voting:

\begin{definition}[Conviction]
The conviction of participant $g_i$ for proposal $p$ at time $t$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Conv}_i^p(t) = \tau_i \cdot \left(1 - e^{-\alpha (t - t_0)}\right)
\end{equation}
where $t_0$ is when $g_i$ started supporting $p$ and $\alpha$ is the decay parameter.
\end{definition}

Total conviction for proposal $p$:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Conv}^p(t) = \sum_{i: \text{supports}(g_i, p)} \text{Conv}_i^p(t)
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[Funding Threshold]
Proposal $p$ requesting amount $a$ is funded when:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Conv}^p(t) \geq \theta(a) = \frac{a \cdot S^2}{(1 - a/V)^2}
\end{equation}
where $S$ is total token supply and $V$ is available treasury.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Conviction Voting Properties]
Conviction voting satisfies:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Time preference}: Longer commitment yields more influence
    \item \textbf{Gradual activation}: Proposals cannot pass immediately
    \item \textbf{Capital efficiency}: Tokens can support multiple proposals
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(1) $\frac{\partial \text{Conv}}{\partial t} > 0$ for $t > t_0$.

(2) At $t = t_0$, $\text{Conv} = 0 < \theta(a)$ for any $a > 0$.

(3) Participant can allocate tokens across proposals; conviction builds independently.
\end{proof}

\section{Spending Proposal System}

\subsection{Proposal Lifecycle}

Spending proposals follow a multi-stage process:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Draft}: Proposer creates specification
    \item \textbf{Temperature Check}: Non-binding sentiment poll
    \item \textbf{Formal Proposal}: Binding governance vote
    \item \textbf{Execution}: Funds disbursed
    \item \textbf{Accountability}: Milestone verification
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Proposal Requirements}

\begin{definition}[Valid Proposal]
A spending proposal is valid if:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\text{amount} \leq \text{spendingCap}$
    \item $\text{purpose} \in \text{AllowedCategories}$
    \item $\text{recipient}$ passes compliance check
    \item $\text{milestones}$ are verifiable
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Spending caps prevent treasury depletion:
\begin{equation}
    \text{spendingCap} = \min\left(\beta \cdot V, \gamma \cdot V_{\text{month}}\right)
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ is the per-proposal limit and $\gamma$ is monthly limit.

\subsection{Voting Mechanism}

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Spending Proposal Vote}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Proposal $p$, voters $\mathcal{G}$, quorum $Q$, threshold $\Theta$
\Ensure Approval decision
\State Initialize vote tallies: $\text{yes} \gets 0$, $\text{no} \gets 0$
\For{each voter $g_i$ casting vote $v_i \in \{\text{YES}, \text{NO}, \text{ABSTAIN}\}$}
    \State $w_i \gets \text{VotingPower}(g_i)$
    \If{$v_i = \text{YES}$}
        \State $\text{yes} \gets \text{yes} + w_i$
    \ElsIf{$v_i = \text{NO}$}
        \State $\text{no} \gets \text{no} + w_i$
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\State $\text{total} \gets \text{yes} + \text{no}$
\If{$\text{total} < Q \cdot \sum_i \tau_i$}
    \State \Return FAIL: quorum not met
\EndIf
\If{$\text{yes} / \text{total} > \Theta$}
    \State \Return APPROVE
\Else
    \State \Return REJECT
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Retroactive Public Goods Funding}

We incorporate retroactive funding for completed work:

\begin{definition}[Retroactive Funding]
Retroactive funding rewards projects based on demonstrated impact:
\begin{equation}
    R_j = f(\text{Impact}_j) \cdot M_{\text{retro}}
\end{equation}
where $\text{Impact}_j$ is assessed post-hoc and $M_{\text{retro}}$ is the retroactive pool.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Incentive Alignment]
Retroactive funding incentivizes impact over promises when:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}[R | \text{high impact}] > \mathbb{E}[R | \text{low impact}] + \text{Cost}(\text{high impact}) - \text{Cost}(\text{low impact})
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Milestone-Based Disbursement}

Large grants use milestone-based release:

\begin{definition}[Milestone Schedule]
A milestone schedule $\{(m_1, a_1, d_1), \ldots, (m_k, a_k, d_k)\}$ specifies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $m_i$: milestone deliverable
    \item $a_i$: amount released upon completion
    \item $d_i$: deadline
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Milestone Verification}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Grant $g$, milestone $m_i$, evidence $e$
\Ensure Release or rejection
\If{$\text{Deadline}(m_i) < \text{now}$}
    \State \Return EXPIRED
\EndIf
\State $\text{reviewers} \gets \text{SelectReviewers}(g.\text{category})$
\State $\text{score} \gets \text{MedianScore}(\text{reviewers}, e)$
\If{$\text{score} \geq \text{passThreshold}$}
    \State $\text{Release}(g.\text{recipient}, a_i)$
    \State \Return APPROVED
\Else
    \State \Return REJECTED
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Treasury Diversification}

\subsection{Portfolio Model}

\begin{definition}[Treasury Portfolio]
The treasury portfolio is allocation $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, \ldots, w_k)$ where $w_i$ is fraction in asset class $i$ and $\sum_i w_i = 1$.
\end{definition}

Asset classes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Native token (LUX)
    \item Stablecoins (USDC, DAI)
    \item Blue-chip crypto (BTC, ETH)
    \item Yield-bearing positions
    \item Protocol-owned liquidity
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Mean-Variance Optimization}

\begin{definition}[Efficient Frontier]
The efficient frontier is the set of portfolios maximizing expected return for given risk:
\begin{equation}
    \max_{\mathbf{w}} \mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\mu} - \frac{\lambda}{2} \mathbf{w}^T \Sigma \mathbf{w}
\end{equation}
subject to $\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{1} = 1$, $w_i \geq 0$, where $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is expected returns and $\Sigma$ is covariance matrix.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Optimal Portfolio]
The optimal portfolio for risk aversion $\lambda$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{w}^* = \frac{1}{\lambda} \Sigma^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu} + \frac{1 - \frac{1}{\lambda} \mathbf{1}^T \Sigma^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu}}{\mathbf{1}^T \Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{1}} \Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{1}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Crypto-Specific Constraints}

Standard portfolio theory requires adaptation:

\begin{definition}[Liquidity Constraint]
Asset $i$ has liquidity depth $L_i$. Maximum allocation:
\begin{equation}
    w_i \leq \frac{L_i \cdot \text{slippageTolerance}}{V}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Concentration Limit]
No single asset exceeds concentration limit $\alpha$:
\begin{equation}
    w_i \leq \alpha \quad \forall i
\end{equation}
Exception: native token may exceed during transition periods.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Stablecoin Floor]
Minimum stablecoin allocation ensures operational runway:
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i \in \text{stable}} w_i \geq \frac{\text{AnnualBurn}}{V}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\subsection{Drawdown Risk Management}

\begin{definition}[Value at Risk]
The $\alpha$-VaR is the maximum loss at confidence level $1 - \alpha$:
\begin{equation}
    \text{VaR}_\alpha = -\inf\{x : P(R \leq x) \leq \alpha\}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Conditional VaR]
The conditional VaR (expected shortfall) is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{CVaR}_\alpha = -\mathbb{E}[R | R \leq -\text{VaR}_\alpha]
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Drawdown Bound]
With diversification constraint $\alpha$ and asset volatilities $\sigma_i$:
\begin{equation}
    \text{CVaR}_{0.05} \leq \alpha \cdot \max_i \sigma_i \cdot 2.33 + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \sigma_p \cdot 2.33
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_p$ is portfolio volatility.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Rebalancing Protocol}

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Treasury Rebalancing}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Current allocation $\mathbf{w}$, target $\mathbf{w}^*$, threshold $\delta$
\Ensure Rebalancing trades
\State $\Delta \gets \mathbf{w}^* - \mathbf{w}$
\If{$\|\Delta\|_\infty < \delta$}
    \State \Return NO\_ACTION
\EndIf
\For{each asset $i$ with $|\Delta_i| > \delta$}
    \State $\text{trade}_i \gets \Delta_i \cdot V$
    \State Schedule trade with TWAP execution
\EndFor
\State Submit rebalancing proposal
\State \Return trades
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Rebalancing uses time-weighted average price (TWAP) execution to minimize market impact:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Slippage} \approx \frac{\text{TradeSize}}{L} \cdot \text{ImpactFactor}
\end{equation}

\section{Game-Theoretic Analysis}

\subsection{Budget Balance}

\begin{theorem}[Budget Balance]
The treasury mechanism is budget balanced:
\begin{equation}
    \sum_t \text{Inflows}(t) - \sum_t \text{Outflows}(t) \geq 0
\end{equation}
when spending caps are enforced.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Monthly spending bounded by $\gamma \cdot V_{\text{month}}$.
Treasury inflows from protocol fees: $\text{Inflow}(t) = \text{Fees}(t)$.

With $\gamma < 1$ and positive fees, treasury maintains solvency:
\begin{equation}
    V(t+1) = V(t) + \text{Fees}(t) - \text{Spending}(t) \geq V(t)(1 - \gamma) > 0
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\subsection{Individual Rationality}

\begin{theorem}[Participation Incentive]
Governance participation is individually rational when:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}[\text{ValueIncrease}] > \text{ParticipationCost}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Token holders benefit from effective treasury management through:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Protocol development funding
    \item Ecosystem growth
    \item Buy pressure from treasury operations
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Incentive Compatibility}

\begin{proposition}[Approximate Truthfulness]
Under quadratic funding with identity verification, truthful contribution is approximately optimal:
\begin{equation}
    u_i(\text{truth}) \geq u_i(\text{misreport}) - \epsilon
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon$ depends on verification costs and collusion difficulty.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Attack Resistance}

\begin{definition}[Treasury Raid]
A treasury raid is a coordinated attack where adversary:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Accumulates governance tokens
    \item Submits proposal extracting treasury value
    \item Votes to approve
    \item Sells tokens after extraction
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Raid Resistance]
With spending cap $\beta$, quorum $Q$, and token price $p$:
\begin{equation}
    \text{AttackCost} = Q \cdot S \cdot p > \beta \cdot V
\end{equation}
implies raid is unprofitable.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Attacker must acquire $Q \cdot S$ tokens at cost $Q \cdot S \cdot p$.
Maximum extraction is $\beta \cdot V$.
Attack profitable only if $\beta \cdot V > Q \cdot S \cdot p$.

With $Q = 0.2$, $\beta = 0.1$, and $V = S \cdot p$:
\begin{equation}
    0.1 \cdot S \cdot p < 0.2 \cdot S \cdot p
\end{equation}
Attack is unprofitable.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Ragequit Protection}

\begin{definition}[Ragequit]
Ragequit allows dissenting token holders to exit with proportional treasury share before approved proposal executes.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Minority Protection]
With ragequit mechanism, minority holders can exit at:
\begin{equation}
    \text{ExitValue}_i = \tau_i \cdot \frac{V - L}{S}
\end{equation}
protecting against adverse governance decisions.
\end{proposition}
