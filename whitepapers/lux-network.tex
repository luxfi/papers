\section{Primary Network Consensus}

Primary Network consensus function by repeated sampling of the network. Each node polls a tiny, constant-sized, randomly selected set of neighbors and modifies its suggestion if a supermajority prefers an alternative value. Samples are repeated until convergence, which occurs swiftly in typical operations, is obtained. By way of illustration, we explain the method of action. A user generates and sends a transaction to a validating node, which is a node participating in the consensus procedure. It is then transmitted to other network nodes via gossiping. What happens if the user also conducts a competing transaction, sometimes known as a double-spend.


To pick between conflicting transactions and prevent double-spending, each node randomly selects a small sample of nodes and queries which of the conflicting transactions the questioned nodes deem to be genuine. If the querying node obtains a response with a supermajority in favor of one transaction, the node modifies its own response to that transaction. Each network node repeats this method until the entire network reaches consensus on one of the competing transactions. Surprisingly, despite the simplicity of the underlying mechanism of operation, these protocols result in very desirable system dynamics that make them appropriate for large-scale implementation.

\subsection{Construction of a Subnet}

A subnet or sub-network is a dynamic group of validators working together to achieve consensus on the state change of multiple blockchains. A single subnet can validate any number of blockchains. The subnet concept offers numerous advantages: If a validator does not care about the blockchains in a particular subnet, it will not join that subnet.


This reduces network traffic and the computational resources required by validators. \subsection{Sybil Control and Membership}

Consensus protocols provide their security guarantees based on the premise that up to a predetermined number of system members may be adversarial. A Sybil attack, in which a node floods the network with malicious identities on the cheap, can easily nullify these guarantees. Fundamentally, the only way to deter such an attack is to trade presence for proof of a difficult-to-fake resource. Past systems have investigated the employment of Sybil deterrence techniques like proof-of-work (PoW), proof-of-stake (PoS), proof-of-elapsed time (POET), proof-of-space and time (PoST), and proof-of-authority (PoA) (PoA). All of these methods, at their heart, serve the same purpose: they demand that each member have some "skin in the game" in the form of an economic commitment, which creates an economic deterrent against misconduct. All involve some form of stake, be it mining rigs and hash power (PoW), disk space (PoST), trusted hardware (POET), or an authorized identity (PoA). This stake is the basis for the economic cost that participants must endure in order to obtain a voice. In Bitcoin, for instance, the ability to contribute valid blocks is directly proportionate to the participant's hash rate.


Unfortunately, there has also been some confusion regarding the distinction between consensus protocols and Sybil control techniques. We observe that the selection of consensus protocols is largely orthogonal to the selection of the Sybil control mechanism. This does not imply that Sybil control techniques are interchangeable, as a specific choice may have ramifications for the underlying guarantees of the consensus protocol.


However, many of these recognized processes can be linked with the Lux* family without much alteration. Lux chose PoS over the basic Sybil control method for security and to ensure that the incentives of players are matched for the benefit of the network. Some forms of stake are naturally centralized: mining rig production (PoW), for example, is inherently centralized in the hands of a few individuals with the necessary expertise and access to the hundreds of patents necessary for competitive VLSI manufacturing. In addition, PoW mining loses value due to the substantial annual miner subsidies. Similarly, major datacenter operators possess the greatest amount of disk space. Moreover, all sybil control systems that incur continuing expenses, such as electricity costs for hashing, drain value from the ecosystem and are environmentally destructive. This, in turn, reduces the feasibility envelope for the token, such that a rapid decline in price can render the system unusable. Proof-of-work automatically favors miners with access to cheap electricity, which has little to do with their capacity to serialize transactions or their contribution to the ecosystem as a whole. We select proof-of-stake because it is eco-friendly, accessible, and available to everyone. We note, however, that while the Lux Network utilizes PoS, subnets can be launched using both PoW and PoS. Staking is a logical method for participation in an open network because it permits a direct economic argument: the likelihood of an attack's success is directly proportional to a well-defined monetary cost function. In other words, nodes that stake have an economic incentive to refrain from engaging in actions that could diminish the value of their stake. In addition, this investment incurs no additional maintenance costs (other than the opportunity cost of investing in another asset) and, unlike mining equipment, is completely consumed in the event of a catastrophic attack. For PoW activities, mining equipment can be easily repurposed or -- if the owner desires to -- totally sold back to the market. A node that wishes to join the network may do so without cost by placing a stake that remains immobilized for the duration of its membership. The user selects the duration and amount of the wager. Once a wager is accepted, it cannot be retracted. The primary objective is to ensuring that nodes have a consistent, stable view of the network. We anticipate establishing a minimum staking period of around one week.


Lux Network, unlike other systems that also provide a PoS approach, provided a slashing protocol for bad actors with slashing recall through DAO committee voting, therefore all stakes incentivize good acting while preventing undesirable occurrences, such as client software or hardware failure resulting in coin loss, which can be reverse by the DAO committee. This aligns with our design philosophy of constructing predictable technology: staked tokens are not at risk despite software or hardware defects. A node that wishes to participate in Lux issues a unique stake transaction to the validator chain. Staking transactions provide a staked amount, the staking key of the participant staking, the duration, and the moment at which validation will commence. Once the transaction is accepted, the money will be frozen until the end of the staking period. The system determines and enforces the lowest permissible amount. The stake quantity deposited by a participant affects both the player's influence in the Lux Platform consensus process and the participant's payout, as will be detailed in greater detail below. The stake duration must be between min and max, the minimum and maximum durations for which any bet can be locked. Similarly to the stake size, the stake period has an effect on the system's reward. Loss or theft of the staking key cannot result in asset loss because it is only utilized for the consensus process and not for asset transfer.

\subsection{Atomic Swaps}

The Lux platform can handle trustless atomic swaps, enabling native, completely decentralized exchanges of any asset type directly on Lux. An atomic swap allows for exchange of one cryptocurrency for another without the need for a trusted third party.


For example, Alice may send BTC to Bob, while Bob sends LUX to Alice. However, as both blockchains are heterogeneous and transactions can't be undone after block consolidation, this operation introduces the counterparty risk problem - i.e.: Alice or Bob fails to honor their end of the deal.


Atomic swaps between CopySwap and BTC involve each party paying into a locked account. In the case of CopySwap, it's a multi-signature account. In the case of BTC, it's an UTXO with a scripted lock.


The creator of Litecoin, Charlie Lee, successfully implemented and demonstrated atomic swaps using LTC in exchange for BTC, Vertcoin and Decred. However, this type of swap only works between the BTC-like chains with similar scripting systems, as well as support for Check Lock Time Verification (CLTV) functionality.


With CLTV support, lock time windows can be set for the refund operation. The initiator sets a 48 hour lock time for participator payment and withdrawal. The participator sets a 24 hour lock time for initiator withdrawal. This time-locked refund scheme guarantees the atomic integrity that any party can withdraw their funds completely when the other party quits the swap process.


Because CopySwap and the LUX Protocol do not have the BTC-like scripting system, instead they deploy a multi-signature account to lock the LUX Tokens the initiator pays to the participator, and CLTV scripting to lock the BTC the participator pays to the initiator.


One drawback of this mechanism is the fact that the multi-signature method cannot implement a recovery and refund process, and CopySwap resolves this with a requested deposit to incentivize and guarantee the trade's integrity.

\section{Voting Based on Reputation and Weighting}

\subsection{Why Use Reputation and Weighted Voting?}

\subsubsection{Rational Indifference}

If tokens held = voting power, then DAO members with less tokens may have fewer incentives to participate in governance. A person who believes their influence on the result of a proposition to be negligible may rationalize their unwillingness to vote.


The phenomenon known as "rational indifference" can deter DAO members from active involvement, putting token whales in charge of governance. This reintroduces the principal-agent problem and undermines the core value proposition of DAOs, which is to place power in the hands of all stakeholders.


This creates an issue that allows crypto whales and other outsiders purchasing governance tokens to exert disproportionate influence in a DAO. In this situation, one individual uninterested in the long-term survival of the group can amass sufficient influence to destabilize it for personal gain.


The Lux DAO employs weighted and reputation-based voting techniques to prevent this issue. These two notions share some similarities, but they are not identical.


\subsubsection{Weighted Voting Systems}

In weighted voting systems, the voting power of tokens is proportional to the length of time they have been kept. Weighted voting systems motivate token holders to lock up their tokens in order to increase the value of their votes.


In a DAO, weighted voting techniques make it more difficult to acquire voting power. You cannot just borrow governance tokens from the market and use them to quickly affect vote outcomes. This will not completely eliminate governance assaults, but it will considerably increase the expense of performing one.


\subsection{Reputation}

The voting mechanism based on reputation follows a similar trend. Members of the DAO receive a single, non-transferable token for voting on proposals. And the voting power associated with each token is tied to the holder's reputation.


Reputation points can be acquired in various ways. A DAO may award reputation points to members based on their membership history. Or, members can gain extra reputation points by positively contributing to the expansion of the organization (for example, by completing tasks as well as participating in community discussions and receiving upvotes for comments on proposals).


Voting based on a candidate's reputation helps ensure that individuals with a lengthy history of involvement in an organization attain positions of prominence. This indicates that it will be difficult for an outsider to enter the Lux DAO through the community governance processes.


\paragraph{Unfair Representation}

In rare cases, reputation voting may be judged unfair. What if, for instance, a respectable member suddenly becomes evil and utilizes their influence inside the group for nefarious purposes? Wouldn't reputation systems merely revive the tragedy of the commons?


The fact that someone earned reputation points in the early stages of a project does not imply that they should always have leadership status. It would be unfair for genesis members to have greater authority than new, active members, especially if their contribution decreases.


\subsection{Slashing and Expiration}

Lux proposes two solutions to this issue. First, DAOs may permit the revocation of reputation points. If a member commits wrongdoing, their reputation points should decrease accordingly.


Second, it is possible to program reputation points to decrease over time or even expire. This solves the problem of injustice and maintains the fluidity of power in DAOs.


However, conventional reputation systems have additional drawbacks such as Anyone can transfer their reputation by selling their private keys (reputation points are linked to wallets). Despite the difficulty of establishing a healthy market for the selling of reputation points, this mechanism is viable for supporting competent DAO governance. To mitigate against this type of attack on the system, Lux Dao requires each voting participant to verify the authentication of their identity through the Lux Decentralized Identification service.


\section{Quadratic Voting}

Quadratic voting is an innovative governance system that has the endorsement of Ethereum's creator Vitalik Buterin. Quadratic voting is attractive because it provides an alternative to token holder voting and lessens the impact of whales on voting outcomes.


In a quadratic voting system, each individual has one vote on a proposition. However, users may purchase additional "credits" in order to cast further votes for their favorite alternative.


When comparing quadratic voting systems to conventional POS where The cost of additional votes is linear in a 1T1V system, quadratic systems are much different. In a one token / one vote system, If you need five additional votes, you simply need five additional tokens. The cost of extra votes in a quadratic voting system is quadratic; one vote costs one token, two votes cost four tokens, three votes cost nine tokens, four votes cost sixteen tokens and so on and so forth.


Individuals can express their level of support for a proposal by purchasing vote credits through quadratic voting. This makes quadratic voting the equivalent of "putting your money where your mouth is" within the DAO.


Concurrently, it decreases the impact of crypto whales on decision-making. It is evident from our earlier illustration that the usefulness of more voting diminishes while the costs increase. A bad actor within a DAO would have to spend a lot of money to obtain enough tokens to manipulate outcomes.

Numerous blockchain platforms, particularly those utilizing Nakamoto consensus like Bitcoin, are afflicted with perpetual state expansion. \subsection{Realistic Adversaries}

In the entire point-to-point paradigm, the Lux whitepaper gives very strong assurances in the presence of a powerful and hostile adversary, known as a round-adaptive adversary. In other words, the adversary has complete access to the state of each and every correct node at all times, is aware of the random selections of all correct nodes, and can update its own state at any time, both before and after the correct node has the opportunity to update its own state. Except for the capacity to directly update the state of a correct node or alter communication between correct nodes, this adversary is effectively all-powerful. Practical implementations of the strongest possible adversary are confined to statistical approximations of the network state, therefore such an opponent exists only in theory. Therefore, we anticipate that worst-case scenario attacks will be difficult to execute in practice.
