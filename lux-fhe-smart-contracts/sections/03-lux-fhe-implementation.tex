\section{Lux FHE Implementation}

\label{sec:implementation}

\subsection{Design Principles}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{Native Go}: The entire FHE backend is implemented in Go using the
        \texttt{luxfi/lattice} library. No Rust FFI, no C bindings, no external
        dependencies (not tfhe-rs, not Zama's fhevm).
  \item \textbf{NTT SIMD}: Polynomial multiplication uses the Number Theoretic
        Transform with AVX-512 SIMD intrinsics via Go assembly. NTT reduces
        $O(N^2)$ polynomial multiplication to $O(N \log N)$.
  \item \textbf{Deterministic gas}: Every FHE operation has a fixed gas cost
        proportional to its computational overhead, enabling predictable transaction
        pricing.
  \item \textbf{Scheme selection}: We support two FHE schemes:
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textbf{TFHE} (Torus FHE): Boolean circuits. 12\,ms per bootstrapped
                gate. Best for comparisons, conditionals, bit-level operations.
          \item \textbf{BFV} (Brakerski-Fan-Vercauteren): Batched integer arithmetic.
                45\,ms for 16-bit addition. Best for arithmetic-heavy computations
                (balance updates, aggregations).
        \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{NTT Acceleration}

The Number Theoretic Transform is the computational bottleneck of lattice-based FHE.
For polynomials in $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^N + 1)$ with $N = 4096$ (BFV) or
$N = 1024$ (TFHE), NTT transforms a polynomial from coefficient representation to
evaluation representation in $O(N \log N)$ modular multiplications.

Our SIMD implementation processes 8 modular multiplications in parallel using AVX-512
\texttt{vpmuludq} instructions. For $N = 4096$:

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\caption{NTT performance (single core, AMD EPYC 7763).}
\label{tab:ntt-benchmarks}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Ring Size $N$} & \textbf{Scalar NTT} & \textbf{SIMD NTT} \\
\midrule
1{,}024 & 18\,$\mu$s & 3.2\,$\mu$s \\
2{,}048 & 42\,$\mu$s & 7.1\,$\mu$s \\
4{,}096 & 98\,$\mu$s & 15.4\,$\mu$s \\
8{,}192 & 225\,$\mu$s & 34\,$\mu$s \\
16{,}384 & 520\,$\mu$s & 78\,$\mu$s \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The SIMD speedup is approximately $5.6\text{--}6.7\times$, consistent with the
theoretical maximum of $8\times$ for 8-wide SIMD with memory access overhead.
