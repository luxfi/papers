\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Post-quantum threshold signatures are the load-bearing primitive of any
blockchain consensus design that wants to survive a cryptographically
relevant quantum adversary. The classical workhorses --- BLS aggregate
\cite{bls,boneh2001short} and ECDSA with CGGMP21
\cite{cggmp21} --- fail catastrophically under Shor's algorithm, and the
threshold replacements available for the post-quantum era do not all
ground their security in the same underlying mathematical assumption.
Defense in depth at the cryptographic primitive layer requires not just
``a PQ scheme,'' but a portfolio of PQ schemes whose security
assumptions are \emph{algorithmically independent}: a break of any one
family does not propagate to the others.

The Lux Polaris cert profile \cite{lp020} encodes this discipline in
production. Polaris is the maximum-assurance Quasar finality profile
that pairs three independent threshold lanes:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Pulsar} --- threshold Module-LWE
        \cite{lp073pulsarpaper,boschini2024corona}, the lattice lane in
        $\mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^{256}+1)$ with a 48-bit prime;
  \item \textbf{Corona} --- threshold Ring-LWE
        \cite{lp104corona,boschini2024corona}, a structurally distinct
        lattice instantiation that does not share Pulsar's module
        parameter choice; and
  \item \textbf{Magnetar} (this paper) --- threshold FIPS 205 SLH-DSA
        \cite{fips205}, the hash-based lane whose security rests on
        SHA-3 / Keccak collision resistance and preimage resistance
        rather than on any structured lattice assumption.
\end{itemize}
The three legs are deliberately drawn from \emph{different mathematical
families}: a polynomial-time algorithm against module-lattice problems
would break Pulsar and Corona simultaneously but leave Magnetar intact;
a polynomial-time preimage attack on Keccak would break Magnetar but
leave the lattice lanes intact; a single quantum oracle that defeats
both families is, on current evidence, an inconsistent ask. Polaris
finality requires \emph{all three} lanes to verify, so an adversary must
break the conjunction --- a strictly harder task than breaking any
single primitive.

Magnetar is the third leg. This paper specifies the construction at
\texttt{github.com/luxfi/magnetar} v0.2.0 (Tier B; ``library available,
formal proofs and independent audit on the Tier A roadmap''), the
security argument under the v0.1 trust caveat, and the path to Tier A.

\subsection{What Magnetar is}
\label{sec:intro:what}

Magnetar v0.1 is a threshold signature scheme for FIPS 205 SLH-DSA
\cite{fips205}. SLH-DSA is the NIST hash-based signature standard
(formerly SPHINCS+ \cite{sphincs}); it is stateless, requires no
trapdoor or structured ring, and inherits its security from the standard
properties of cryptographic hash functions (collision resistance,
preimage resistance, second-preimage resistance, and the
Boneh--Shoup \cite{boneh-shoup-textbook} sub-exponential preimage bound
under quantum adversaries).

The Magnetar construction departs structurally from the lattice-family
threshold templates of FROST \cite{frost,rfc9591} or Pulsar
\cite{lp073pulsarpaper}. SLH-DSA has \emph{no linear share-aggregation
identity}: there is no algebraic operation analogous to FROST's
$z = \sum_i z_i$ that produces a valid SLH-DSA signature from per-party
contributions on the same message. A direct MPC over the SLH-DSA hash
tree (the v0.2 research target of \S\ref{sec:fullmpc}) is possible in
principle but unsubtle in practice: it would require distributed
generation of every WOTS+ hash chain and every Merkle authentication
path, with cost proportional to the entire SLH-DSA signing transcript
($\sim 10^4$ hash invocations for SHAKE-192s).

Magnetar v0.1 takes a different route. We share the \emph{SLH-DSA scheme
seed} (the input to FIPS 205 $\textsc{KeyGen}$ / $\textsc{DeriveKey}$),
not the resulting signature. The scheme seed is a 96-byte buffer
(192s/192f) or 128-byte buffer (256s); it is the unique secret input
from which the full SLH-DSA secret key, public key, and every signature
derive deterministically. Sharing the seed byte-wise via Shamir over
$\mathrm{GF}(257)$ admits the standard Lagrange reconstruction at $x=0$
to recover the seed inside the aggregator; the aggregator then invokes
the single-party FIPS 205 $\textsc{SignDeterministic}$ on the
reconstructed seed and zeroizes the secret material before return.

The honest characterisation: \emph{the cryptographic primitive is FIPS
205 SLH-DSA; the threshold layer is a byte-wise Shamir VSS over the
scheme seed; the trust model is reveal-and-aggregate.} The aggregator
process is in the TCB for the brief window during which the seed is
reconstructed in memory. This is the same trust model Pulsar v0.1 and
Corona v0.1 ship, and is documented operationally in
\texttt{DEPLOYMENT-RUNBOOK.md}.

\subsection{Why hash-based}
\label{sec:intro:whyhash}

Lattice cryptography (Module-LWE, Ring-LWE, NTRU) is the consensus
post-quantum direction for performance reasons --- ML-KEM
\cite{fips203}, ML-DSA \cite{fips204}, and FALCON \cite{falcon} all
operate in milliseconds, ship signatures in the few-KB range, and
parallelise well. But the lattice family rests on a single set of
related-problem assumptions (Module-LWE, Ring-LWE, NTRU, Module-SIS)
whose hardness is conjectured but whose ultimate security margin is the
subject of ongoing cryptanalytic refinement. The cryptanalytic surface
includes BKZ block-size cost models \cite{bdgl2016,laarhoven2015}, dual
sieves \cite{albrechtestimator}, structured-lattice algebraic attacks
on cyclotomic rings, and quantum-amplified sieves whose constants
remain in modest motion. A polynomial-time algorithm for any of these
problems would invalidate the entire lattice signature ecosystem
simultaneously.

Hash-based signatures rest on assumptions that are \emph{older, more
broadly studied, and structurally orthogonal}. FIPS 205 SLH-DSA's
security reduces to:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,nosep]
  \item collision resistance of the hash function family
        (SHA-3 / SHAKE for the FIPS 205 SHAKE modes Magnetar targets);
  \item preimage resistance of the same;
  \item the absence of unforeseen structural weaknesses in the WOTS+
        and FORS one-time / few-time signature schemes that build on
        the hash function.
\end{itemize}
These properties have been studied for decades. SHA-3 in particular
emerged from a multi-year open competition \cite{keccak-keccak}; its
internal structure (Keccak's sponge construction over a permutation)
is qualitatively distinct from Merkle--Damg{\aa}rd, BLAKE, and the
keyed-permutation Romulus and Ascon families. A quantum attack that
defeats SHA-3 is one that defeats hash functions \emph{as a class}, a
strictly stronger ask than defeating any one family.

The trade-off is performance: SLH-DSA signatures are 16--36~KB, signing
takes hundreds of milliseconds on commodity hardware, and the
construction is the slowest of the FIPS-standardised PQ schemes. For a
\emph{single} hot-path signature this would be a non-starter. For the
\emph{Polaris third lane} it is precisely the trade-off we want:
Polaris's BLS lane sets the latency floor, Pulsar sets the per-block PQ
floor, and Magnetar shoulders the diversity insurance --- it does not
need to be the fastest, only to be \emph{independent} of the other two.

\subsection{What is novel}
\label{sec:intro:novel}

The v0.1 cryptographic primitives are not new. Shamir
\cite{shamir1979} introduced byte-wise secret sharing over a small
prime in 1979; the construction has been textbook material since the
1980s \cite{katzlindell}. FIPS 205 \cite{fips205} is the
NIST-standardised SLH-DSA. The reveal-and-aggregate threshold pattern
(``share the seed, reconstruct in memory, call the centralised
primitive'') is itself an obvious construction and has been described
in the threshold-cryptography folklore for ages.

What \texttt{luxfi/magnetar} contributes is the \emph{composition}:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,nosep]
  \item A Class-N1-analog claim and its empirical validation
        ($\textsc{TestN1\_ByteEquality}$, end-to-end byte equality
        between the threshold and centralised paths across three
        $(n,t)$ configurations).
  \item A KAT-deterministic test discipline: every secret-bearing
        component (Shamir polynomial, mask, commit) is derivable from
        a single 48-byte master seed via SHA-256 chaining, so the
        full submission-grade KAT package is reproducible
        bit-identically from a clean checkout.
  \item Hash-function domain separation across every primitive ---
        cSHAKE256 with function name ``Magnetar'' and per-purpose
        customisation strings (\texttt{MAGNETAR-DKG-COMMIT-V1},
        \texttt{MAGNETAR-SIGN-R1-V1}, \texttt{MAGNETAR-SEED-SHARE-V1},
        etc.) so cross-context replay attacks reduce to cSHAKE256
        collision resistance.
  \item Honest operational disclosure: the v0.1 reveal-and-aggregate
        trust caveat is documented at the construction-specification
        level, at the deployment-runbook level, and at the proof-claim
        level. No covert reliance on aggregator integrity; no hidden
        trust assumptions.
  \item A Polaris cert-profile composition: Magnetar joins Pulsar and
        Corona as the third lane of the maximum-assurance profile,
        sharing the LSS lifecycle adapter contract \cite{luxlssmpc}
        and the Prism transcript-binding discipline \cite{lp073pulsarpaper}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Trust model summary}
\label{sec:intro:trust}

We state the trust model at the outset; it is the dominant honest
caveat of v0.1.

\paragraph{In the TCB at sign time:} the aggregator process. The
aggregator reconstructs the master seed $S$ in memory at step 6 of
$\textsc{Combine}$ (\S\ref{sec:construction:combine}), invokes
$\textsc{SignDeterministic}$, and zeroizes $S$ before return. During
the $\sim 1$--$50$~ms window between reconstruction and zeroization
(SLH-DSA SHAKE-192s signing latency on M2 Pro is on the order of
$10$~ms), an adversary with arbitrary code-execution rights on the
aggregator host can extract $S$ via the standard kernel-level vectors
(\texttt{/proc/<pid>/mem}, ptrace, coredumps, swap-file leaks).

\paragraph{NOT in the TCB:} a passive network observer (Round-1 commits
hide $r_i, s'_i$ behind cSHAKE256; v0.1 envelopes are plaintext, which
v0.2 closes via ML-KEM-768 wrapping). A \emph{single} Byzantine
committee member (forging requires reconstructing $S$, which requires
$t$ shares). A Byzantine aggregator that has not received valid
Round-2 reveals (the commit-bind check in $\textsc{Combine}$ step 1a
rejects tampered reveals).

\paragraph{Mitigations.} The aggregator should run in a memory-encrypted
VM (AMD SEV-SNP, Intel TDX), with coredumps and ptrace disabled, swap
off, on dedicated hardware. The aggregator process should be ephemeral:
\texttt{fork()} per signature, exit after $\textsc{Combine}$ returns.
\texttt{DEPLOYMENT-RUNBOOK.md} \S``Aggregator hardening'' lists the
production checklist.

\paragraph{Tier A path.} v0.2 (research target;
\S\ref{sec:fullmpc}) replaces the reveal-and-aggregate aggregator with
a per-signature MPC over the SLH-DSA hash tree. This eliminates the
aggregator-TCB caveat at the cost of substantially higher per-signature
communication. The construction is nontrivial because SLH-DSA has no
linear share-aggregation identity; the v0.2 design surveys the
candidate MPC topologies in \S\ref{sec:fullmpc:topology}.

\subsection{What this paper specifies}
\label{sec:intro:paper}

Section~\ref{sec:preliminaries} reviews FIPS 205 SLH-DSA, byte-wise
Shamir secret sharing over $\mathrm{GF}(257)$, the threshold-signature
syntax, and the security games. Section~\ref{sec:construction} gives
the byte-exact v0.1 reveal-and-aggregate construction in pseudocode,
keyed to the Go canonical at \texttt{ref/go/pkg/magnetar/} byte-for-byte.
Section~\ref{sec:security} states the security argument:
construction-equivalence to centralized FIPS 205 SLH-DSA, the
UF-CMA reduction under the aggregator-honest assumption, and the proof
roadmap for the open obligations. Section~\ref{sec:implementation}
surveys the reference implementation (3\,992 LOC, 12 files).
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} reports parameter sets and per-signature
latency from the production test suite. Section~\ref{sec:comparison}
locates Magnetar alongside Pulsar and Corona in the Polaris cert
profile. Section~\ref{sec:fullmpc} sketches the path to a Tier A v0.2
that closes the aggregator-TCB caveat via a per-signature MPC.
Section~\ref{sec:related} surveys the (sparse) threshold-SLH-DSA
literature and the broader threshold-hash-based-signature work.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} restates the honest Tier B status and the
Tier A path.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Tier B status.} At the time of writing,
Magnetar carries \emph{Tier B} status in the Lux Lyttle tier discipline:
production library available, KAT vectors deterministic, byte-equality
empirically validated, but \emph{no} mechanised proofs and \emph{no}
independent cryptographer audit have landed. The path to Tier A
(mechanised proof artefacts, independent review sign-off) is sketched
in \texttt{BLOCKERS.md} BLK-7 and BLK-9 and in
\S\ref{sec:security:roadmap} below. \emph{Use Magnetar today as a
library at your operational risk}; in the Lux Polaris production
deployment it is one of three independent lanes, so a Magnetar break is
insurance lost, not safety lost.
