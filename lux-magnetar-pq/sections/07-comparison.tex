\section{Comparison to Pulsar and Corona}
\label{sec:comparison}

Magnetar is positioned alongside Pulsar (M-LWE) \cite{lp073pulsarpaper}
and Corona (R-LWE) \cite{lp104corona} as the three independent legs of
the Lux Polaris cert profile. This section makes the comparison
explicit: what is structurally shared, what is structurally distinct,
and why all three are necessary.

\subsection{The shared v0.1 trust caveat}
\label{sec:cmp:caveat}

All three primitives ship v0.1 reveal-and-aggregate constructions in
their first releases. This is not coincidence; it is a deliberate
sequencing decision documented in the Lux PQ roadmap
\cite{lp020}. Each v0.1 trades full MPC for a simpler construction
that:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,nosep]
  \item produces byte-identical output to the underlying centralised
        primitive (the Class-N1 / Class-N1-analog claim), so consuming
        verifiers need no code change;
  \item carries an honest aggregator-TCB caveat, documented in the
        respective \texttt{DEPLOYMENT-RUNBOOK.md} of each repository;
  \item ships first so the protocol composition (Polaris cert) can be
        validated end-to-end before the more complex v0.2 MPC
        constructions land.
\end{enumerate}
The trust caveat is uniform: the aggregator process holds the
reconstructed secret material in memory for the duration of a single
$\textsc{Combine}$ call, with the same threat surface and the same
operational mitigations (memory-encrypted VMs, ephemeral processes,
seccomp-bpf restrictions). Any deployment that runs all three lanes
must apply the hardening checklist to all three aggregator processes;
if any single lane's aggregator is compromised, that lane's PQ
insurance is lost (but the conjunction of the other two lanes still
provides post-quantum safety under Polaris's composition theorem).

\subsection{Mathematical independence}
\label{sec:cmp:independence}

The three primitives rest on \emph{algorithmically distinct} security
assumptions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Pulsar} (M-LWE): security reduces to Module-LWE
        \cite{mlwe,dilithium} on
        $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^{256}+1)$ with $q \approx 2^{48}$.
        The module structure (working over an 8-row $\times$ 7-column
        matrix of polynomials) is specific to the
        Pulsar/Dilithium family.
  \item \textbf{Corona} (R-LWE): security reduces to Ring-LWE
        \cite{regev2009,lp104corona} on
        $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^N+1)$ with $q$ a 48-bit
        NTT-friendly prime. Note: Corona has \emph{no} module
        structure --- it operates on a single ring rather than on a
        vector of polynomials. A break of Module-LWE that exploits
        the module structure does not necessarily transfer to Corona.
  \item \textbf{Magnetar} (SLH-DSA): security reduces to collision
        resistance and preimage resistance of SHA-3 (Keccak)
        \cite{keccak-keccak}, the WOTS+ one-time signature
        construction, and the FORS few-time signature construction
        \cite{sphincs,fips205}. There is no lattice anywhere in
        Magnetar.
\end{itemize}

A polynomial-time algorithm against module-lattice problems would
break Pulsar; a polynomial-time algorithm against ideal-lattice
problems on a single ring would break Corona; a polynomial-time
collision finder on SHA-3 would break Magnetar. These three break
conditions are believed independent. The Polaris finality predicate
requires all three lanes to verify, so a Polaris adversary must
break the conjunction --- strictly harder than breaking any single
primitive.

\paragraph{Why three lanes, not two.} Pulsar and Corona are both
lattice schemes; a single algorithmic break of lattice cryptography
would invalidate both simultaneously. The cryptographic-diversity
argument requires a leg from outside the lattice family. Hash-based
is the natural choice: it is the only \emph{NIST-standardised}
PQ signature family that is not lattice-based, and SLH-DSA's
security assumptions are arguably the best-understood of any
PQ signature scheme.

\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}

\midrule
Pulsar & Module-LWE   & $\sim 250$~ms & $\sim 25$~KB & $\sim 33$~KB \\

\bottomrule

Magnetar's per-signature latency is the slowest of the three; this
is FIPS 205's intrinsic cost. But Magnetar's per-party wire bytes
are by far the smallest --- the threshold layer transmits only a
32-byte commit and a $\sim 400$-byte masked share, because the
SLH-DSA signature itself is computed locally at the aggregator (not
constructed from per-party contributions).

The signature size at $\sim 16$~KB (SHAKE-192s) is competitive: only
$\sim 2\times$ larger than Pulsar's M-LWE signature and
substantially smaller than Corona's R-LWE signature. SHAKE-192f
trades a $\sim 3\times$ larger signature ($35$~KB) for $\sim 10\times$
faster signing, which may be the right trade-off for higher-rate
deployments; this is a per-deployment configuration choice.

\subsection{Lifecycle composition}
\label{sec:cmp:lifecycle}

All three lanes plug into the same LSS \cite{luxlssmpc} lifecycle
adapter:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,nosep]
  \item \texttt{lss/protocols/lss\_pulsar.go} adapts Pulsar's
        $\textsc{Reshare}$ and $\textsc{Refresh}$ to the LSS
        generation-aware orchestration.
  \item \texttt{lss/protocols/lss\_corona.go} adapts Corona's
        R-LWE resharing.
  \item \texttt{lss/protocols/lss\_magnetar.go} (v0.3 target)
        will adapt Magnetar. The Magnetar adapter contract is
        \emph{simpler} than the lattice adapters because the
        Shamir-over-GF(257) sharing is inherently shape-preserving:
        a $\textsc{Reshare}$ from old set $V$ to new set $V'$
        re-Shamir-shares the master byte-sum across $V'$ with the
        same $(t, n)$ structure, and the cSHAKE256 mix
        produces the same master seed when the committee changes.
\end{itemize}

The activation cert composition: a Polaris generation transition
requires three activation certs in lockstep, one per lane. Each
activation cert verifies under the unchanged group public key of
its respective lane (cf. Pulsar Theorem 5.2,
\cite{lp073pulsarpaper}~\S5.2). All three must accept for the
Polaris cert profile to advance.

\subsection{Trust caveat: what differs across lanes}
\label{sec:cmp:caveat-differ}

The aggregator-TCB caveat applies uniformly. The \emph{secret material}
that lives in the aggregator's memory during $\textsc{Combine}$
differs:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Pulsar}: the reconstructed master secret
        $\mathbf{s} \in R_q^{n_{\mathrm{vec}}}$ (a 7-element vector
        of polynomials over $\mathbb{Z}_q$, $q \approx 2^{48}$,
        degree 256). $\sim 11$~KB of secret material.
  \item \textbf{Corona}: the reconstructed R-LWE secret
        $s \in R_q$ (a single polynomial over the cyclotomic ring).
        $\sim 1.5$~KB of secret material.
  \item \textbf{Magnetar}: the reconstructed SLH-DSA scheme seed
        $S$ (96 or 128 bytes) plus the recovered SLH-DSA secret
        key (96 or 128 bytes). $\sim 200$~B of secret material.
\end{itemize}

Magnetar's TCB exposure is the \emph{smallest} of the three lanes in
absolute byte terms: the secret material is two short buffers
totalling $\sim 200$ bytes (for SHAKE-192s) versus Pulsar's
$\sim 11$~KB. This is a modest practical advantage --- a smaller
secret is easier to zeroize reliably and easier to keep in CPU
registers --- but does not change the qualitative caveat.

\subsection{Where each lane is the right answer}
\label{sec:cmp:fit}

For deployments that adopt only a single PQ lane (rather than the
full Polaris three-lane composition), the trade-off:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Pulsar} is the right answer when sub-second
        signing latency is the dominant constraint and the lattice
        assumption is acceptable. Pulsar's per-party wire bandwidth
        is high, but its hot-path latency is the lowest.
  \item \textbf{Corona} is the right answer when validator
        committees scale beyond $K \sim 100$ and the R-LWE single-ring
        structure is preferred to Pulsar's M-LWE module structure
        (some deployments may prefer the simpler ring for audit
        clarity).
  \item \textbf{Magnetar} is the right answer when the
        threat model includes ``the lattice family might be broken
        algorithmically'' (a long-tail risk) or when the deployment
        needs a primitive whose security can be argued from
        hash-function assumptions alone. Magnetar's per-party wire
        bandwidth is by far the smallest, which makes it the
        right answer for bandwidth-constrained deployments
        (cellular / IoT / regulated-traffic environments).
\end{itemize}

For \textbf{maximum assurance} (the Lux Polaris production
deployment), all three lanes run in parallel. The triple-redundancy
is the correct response to the long-tail cryptographic risks of the
PQ transition era.
