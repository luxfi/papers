\section{The DEX Receipt-Settlement Precompile (0x9999)}
\label{sec:dex-adapter}

The twenty precompiles documented above are \emph{cryptographic}
primitives: pure functions of their input bytes with no external state.
Lux adds one precompile of a different kind --- an
\emph{application adapter} --- at address \texttt{0x9999}, in the
application range \texttt{0x9000}--\texttt{0x90ff} reserved above the
cryptographic block. It is the canonical EVM-side entry point to the Lux
DEX, and its engineering is instructive precisely because it is not a
pure function: it is a deterministic \emph{settlement} adapter over a
separate matching chain. The earlier \texttt{0x9010} adapter is
deprecated and retired to a single namespace-shared path; \texttt{0x9999}
is the sole production money path. The normative specification is
LP-9999 \cite{lux-lp9999}; this section documents the EVM-side
engineering and defers the full settlement contract to it.

\subsection{A CLOB Settlement Adapter, Not an AMM and Not a Relay}

\texttt{0x9999} exposes the Uniswap-V4-\texttt{PoolManager} ABI
unchanged --- pool keys, hooks, flash accounting --- but it is neither an
automated market maker nor a live matcher. A call does not move along a
bonding curve, and --- crucially --- it does \emph{not} relay an order to
a matching engine and wait for a fill. The matching engine, the resting
book, and value conservation live entirely on the \textbf{D-Chain} (the
Lux Lightspeed DEX matching VM \cite{lux-lightspeed-dex}). The D-Chain
matches an order and a D-validator quorum BLS-signs a \texttt{DFillReceipt};
\texttt{0x9999} then \emph{settles that receipt} --- it verifies the
certificate and debits/credits EVM balances. The V4-shaped surface is
retained so that existing V4-aware routers and Solidity integrators
compose unchanged, while the execution semantics underneath are
price-time priority CLOB matching certified out-of-band rather than
constant-product swaps.

This is deliberately distinct from the plain Solidity AMMs Lux also
ships: the V2 (constant-product) and V3 (concentrated-liquidity) pools
are ordinary contracts with no precompile involvement. The precompile
exists only for the CLOB path, where on-chain bytecode cannot match a
limit-order book at the throughput the D-Chain achieves natively. The
design slogan is \emph{D matches $\cdot$ C settles $\cdot$ BLS certifies
day-1 $\cdot$ Q later $\cdot$ X finalizes}.

\subsection{The Build-vs-Verify Split (Why Not a Live Relay)}

A live relay is fork-unsafe. If the precompile called a matcher against a
moving book during block execution, every validator would re-execute that
call at \emph{Verify} at a different instant, obtain a different fill, and
compute a different state root --- a consensus split. The resolution is a
strict \textbf{build-vs-verify split} carried by a certificate. On the
\emph{build/propose} path, a node that validates the D-Chain obtains the
fill from its local matcher and assembles a signed \texttt{DFillReceipt}.
On the \emph{verify/replay} path --- every C-Chain validator, inside
\texttt{Run} --- the precompile MUST NOT query any live matcher; it settles
solely from the certificate carried in the V4 \texttt{hookData} (or,
equivalently, the block-header predicate rail). Because certificate
verification is a pure function of the receipt bytes and the verifier key,
it yields an identical result on every validator. This is exactly the
determinism contract every other precompile in this paper honours --- here
achieved not by statelessness but by verifying a certificate rather than
recomputing a fill.

\subsection{Inline BLS Certificate Verification (Deterministic)}

The day-1 certificate is a BLS aggregate signature over a receipt root: a
D-validator quorum signs the per-block (or per-batch) receipt root, and
each C-Chain swap carries the receipt, its Merkle inclusion proof, and one
BLS certificate. Aggregate BLS verification is deterministic and side-effect
free, so it is safe to perform inside \texttt{Run} --- the same pairing
machinery documented in the cryptographic sections above, applied to an
application certificate. The certificate kind is a versioned
\texttt{certType} enum resolved through an on-chain verifier registry, so
the cryptography upgrades with \textbf{no ABI change}: BLS fast-path on
day-1, then QuasarCert, a post-quantum finality profile, or a STARK/zk
batch proof register a new \texttt{certType} at an activation height while
the V4 ABI and the \texttt{DFillReceipt} envelope stay fixed.

\subsection{Native LUX as \texttt{address(0)}}

The pool-key encoding follows the V4 convention that the native asset is
the zero address. A pool key referencing \texttt{address(0)} denotes
native LUX; ERC-20 securities use their token address. This removes the
wrapped-native special case from the adapter ABI: every asset, native or
token, is an address in the pool key, and \texttt{address(0)} routes to
the D-Chain's native-balance path. Internally, settlement binds the full
32-byte \texttt{AssetID} --- never an alias or fold --- so the pool-key
address is resolved to a canonical asset identity before any balance moves.

\subsection{Exactly-Once Settlement via Consumed Receipts}

A settlement precompile faces a hazard a pure precompile does not: the
same EVM call may be re-executed (block re-org, validator restart,
speculative execution) and must not settle the same fill twice. Each
\texttt{DFillReceipt} carries a unique \texttt{receiptID}, and the
precompile records \texttt{consumedReceipt[receiptID]} on success;
re-presenting a consumed receipt reverts. The receipt is further bound to
exactly one swap on exactly one chain: it commits to the pool-key hash,
the swap-parameters hash, the sender, the recipient, the minimum output,
the deadline, and the precompile address \texttt{0x9999}, and the
precompile reverts on any mismatch. A revert means nothing happened --- no
input taken, no output credited, no partial DEX state --- preserving the
all-or-nothing ownership invariant that only the named counterparties move
value. Replay protection is thus state (the consumed-receipt set) rather
than content-addressing, which is what makes settlement, as opposed to a
fire-and-forget relay, safe and idempotent.

\subsection{Block-STM Parallel Settlement}

\texttt{0x9999} is Block-STM-aware. It declares a fine-grained read/write
access set --- the consumed-receipt slot for this receipt, the sender's and
recipient's per-asset balance slots, the relevant pool and asset registry
entries, the verifier-registry entry for the signing validator set, and a
sharded fee bucket --- with \textbf{no global hot write slots} (no global
nonce, volume counter, single fee accumulator, or last-receipt slot). Two
swaps conflict only if they share an account, asset, receipt, allowance, or
pool key, so the universal C-Chain validator set becomes a parallel
settlement fabric: thousands of independent fills settle concurrently and
only genuine conflicts serialize. Trading is stoppable at fine-grained halt
scopes (global, per-market, per-asset, per-receipt-type, per-validator-set),
and the fund-preserving default on an incident is to stop new swaps while
still allowing cancels, settlement of already-certified safe receipts, and
withdrawals.

\subsection{Why This Belongs in the Precompile Layer}

A CLOB cannot be matched in EVM bytecode at competitive cost --- the same
argument that justifies the cryptographic precompiles --- and a BLS
aggregate certificate cannot be verified in bytecode at competitive gas.
The adapter keeps the EVM as the settlement and composition surface
(V4-compatible, hook-friendly) while delegating latency-critical matching
to a chain engineered for it and certificate verification to the precompile
layer. The full D-Chain VM architecture --- matcher-at-\emph{Verify},
\texttt{zapdb} commit, crash-restart durability, and the GPU CLOB matcher
behind it --- is specified in the Lux Lightspeed DEX paper
\cite{lux-lightspeed-dex}, and the receipt-settlement contract --- receipt
binding, the certType verifier registry, the Block-STM access set, halt
scopes, and the \texttt{0x9010} migration --- is normative in LP-9999
\cite{lux-lp9999}. This section documents only the EVM-side adapter and the
engineering discipline (build-vs-verify split, \texttt{address(0)} native,
inline deterministic certificate verification, exactly-once consumed
receipts) that makes a stateful settlement adapter safe to expose as a
precompile.
