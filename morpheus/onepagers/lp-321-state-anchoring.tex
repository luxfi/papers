\proposalhead{Anchoring Marketplace State}{%
Two economies on one emission schedule, replicated by hand. Neither domain's contracts can read the
other, so every claim tying emission to compute demand rests on an off-chain component.}{LP-321}

\noindent\textbf{The split.}
Capital lives on Ethereum, where \code{DistributorV2} splits the period's MOR across deposit pools by
Aave yield. Compute lives on Base, in one EIP-2535 diamond holding its own copy of the schedule: a
\code{Pool[]} array written by \code{setPoolConfig} under \code{onlyOwner}, whose \code{@dev} line
says the values ``should be the same as in Ethereum L1 Distribution contract''
(\code{SessionRouter.sol:57}, \code{SessionStorage.sol:16}). MOR crosses as a LayerZero
OFT and a Python service sits between. Nothing checks the mirror. Nor is either Base payout a mint:
providers get \code{safeTransferFrom(fundingAccount, provider, amount)}
(\code{SessionRouter.sol:397}), builder stakers \code{safeTransferFrom(treasury, staker, toClaim)}
(\code{BuilderSubnets.sol:335}), so payment depends on a balance and an approval held off-chain.
``24\% of emission'' is a policy about a schedule; at the contract level it is a treasury someone
tops up.

\vspace{0.35ex}
\noindent\textbf{The coupling variable, and the mirror's error.}
\code{stakeToStipend} makes purchasable session time a function of \code{getComputeBalance}:
cumulative compute emission minus \code{providersTotalClaimed}, the total drawn
(\code{SessionRouter.sol:183, 392, 484}). That is the only place realised demand feeds back into what
the market can sell, and Ethereum cannot read it. Both domains call the same deployed
\code{LinearDistribution\discretionary{-}{}{}IntervalDecrease} with independently configured
arguments, so relative error $\beta$ in the decrease rate $d$ gives relative error
$\beta dn/(R_0-dn)$ at day $n$, diverging as $R_0-dn\to 0$.
The mirror is least reliable exactly when the pool is smallest.

\vspace{0.35ex}
\noindent\textbf{What must cross, and what must not.}
Minimising the crossed state is the design, so bound it: four scalars and two commitments per UTC
day. Scalars are \code{providersTotalClaimed}, settled session-seconds, sessions closed, and
$\min(\text{balance},\text{allowance})$ of the funding account, the real cap on payable capacity;
commitments are a Merkle root over per-provider claim totals and a digest of the pool config in
force. Excluded: sessions, bids, prices, prompts, model identity, telemetry. A daily digest of fixed
size is the right object, not a message bus.

\vspace{0.35ex}
\noindent\textbf{Verification, three levels, threat model named.}
The adversary is the diamond owner plus the funding-account operator: rewrite the pool config,
repoint \code{fundingAccount}, stop funding, silently. \emph{Attested digest with a challenge window}
is nearly free and guarantees only that a false digest cannot pass unopposed. \emph{Storage proof
against a finalised Base output root} removes the trusted party with machinery Ethereum tooling
ships, at the price of the rollup's fault window. \emph{A native quorum certificate} removes that
window and needs a compute domain that produces one. Lux cross-chain finality applies between Lux
chains, not into a Base diamond; if that is the goal, LP-314 owns it.

\begin{stake}{What Morpheus gets}
\item A mirror that is checked, and drawn compute emission as a number governance reads, not infers.
\item Owed and payable as separate visible quantities, so a shortfall surfaces before a provider does.
\end{stake}

\begin{stake}{What we get, stated plainly}
\item A production anchoring target we did not design, a harder test than our own chains give.
\item Enso routing measured against a demand series with provenance rather than a dashboard.
\end{stake}

\noindent\textbf{The cheap intermediate, and who takes the first step.}
Nothing above needs Lux. Three changes to Base and Ethereum as they stand: a \code{view} returning
\code{keccak256} of the pool config each side holds, so anyone can diff them;
\code{SessionClosed} (\code{ISessionRouter.sol:10}) widened to carry duration and amount, so demand is
reconstructible from logs alone; balance and allowance exposed beside \code{getTodaysBudget}. The
first is the whole first step and it belongs to a Morpheus contributor: add the digest, read
\code{Distribution.pools(3)}, compare. An afternoon, no counterparty, no upgrade beyond a facet, and
either the mirror holds or the divergence is a number. We publish the digest schema and its verifier.

\vspace{0.35ex}
\noindent\textbf{Governance, which is why this matters.}
MRC authorises the 24\% compute allocation, and the figure telling it whether that tracks real demand
is \code{providersTotalClaimed}, in storage MRC cannot see. The last MRC commit is 2025-11-17.
Anchoring does not fix the process; it gives it a number to check.

\vspace{0.35ex}
\noindent\textbf{What this does not solve.}
Anchoring transports the compute domain's accounting faithfully; it does not make it true. A session
an 8B model answered while a 70B was advertised commits like any other, so LP-313 is the
prerequisite. It creates no demand, and a compromised owner can still rewrite state before a digest
is taken.
