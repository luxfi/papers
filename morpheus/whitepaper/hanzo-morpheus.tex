\section{The position today}

Morpheus emits 14{,}400 MOR a day [verified]. Real protocol revenue, the Lido
and Aave yield on staked deposits, is about \$476K a year and falling; at the
2024 launch it was \$3.24M a quarter [verified]. Emission outruns real revenue
by roughly ten to one [verified]. MOR trades at \$1.95, a market capitalization
near \$17M, against on-chain DEX depth of about \$1.4M that is 88.8\%
protocol-owned and held in a single Safe, on 24-hour volume near \$8.9K
[verified]. Seventy-five percent of the yield funds a buyback of about \$511K a
year [verified].

This is not a Ponzi. Principal is withdrawable one to one, and the capital pool
holds about \$20M of third-party stETH [verified]. The problem is narrower and
worse for a token. Two facts set the whole argument.

First, revenue is decoupled from usage. It is yield on deposits, so it scales
with capital and falls with rates, not with inference sold. The one number that
would justify the emission, paid inference, is published nowhere in the 62
repositories [verified]. Eighteen providers are listed on \code{active.mor.org},
largely reselling Venice and OpenAI subscriptions [verified]. No usage, session,
or revenue figure exists anywhere in the tree [verified].

Second, settlement is fakeable. A provider signs a receipt over seven fields
including \code{inputTokens} and \code{outputTokens} (\code{abi.go:12--19})
[verified]. The contract decodes five of them and ignores both token counts and
the model (\code{SessionRouter.sol:236}) [verified]. Payment is
\code{pricePerSecond} times elapsed time. Faked work and a substituted model
settle identically to honest work. There is no slashing, the grep is empty
[verified]; no timelock [verified]; and the token has no mint cap
(\code{MOROFT.sol:61}) [verified]. The owner is an anonymous 5-of-9 [verified].

We are Hanzo AI and Lux Industries. We bring the two things the tree is missing:
demand into the market, and a settlement receipt that cannot be faked. Hanzo
operates an OpenAI-compatible inference endpoint and a learned router that selects
a model per request in microseconds on CPU. Lux operates a blockchain with
post-quantum key-encapsulation exposed as EVM precompiles on mainnet. This
document states Morpheus's problem in Morpheus's numbers, names each fix to a
repository that already exists, and gives one quantified outcome with its
assumptions on the table.

\paragraph{Provenance.} Every number carries a label. [verified] is read from
chain, a repository, or DefiLlama. [illustrative] is modeled from an assumption
stated beside it. [pending] awaits an external approval. [client] is our own
reach. Unlabeled code citations are [verified] against the working trees at
\code{MorpheusAIs/*} as of August 2026.

\subsection{The technical spine}

The economic problem has a technical cause, and it runs in one line. Settlement
pays for time, not tokens. If revenue is fixed at bid time and cost of service is
unobserved, the choice of which model answers a request is the entire margin.
That gap is an arbitrage. Someone will take it. Taking it in the open requires a
provider to declare what it may serve; a declaration is only a statement until
the network can check that the advertised model answered; checking per request
rather than per audit needs verification priced in gas; and no rented chain hosts
a precompile its operator did not deploy. Pricing, routing, disclosure,
verification, and settlement are one chain of implications, not five topics. The
outcome in Section~\ref{sec:outcome} is what closing that chain is worth.

A second chain runs alongside and depends on none of the first. Consumer prompts
cross the wire in cleartext while provider responses are encrypted, so the half
of the exchange that usually carries the sensitive material is the unprotected
half. A captured prompt does not expire, which puts confidentiality on an
unbounded horizon and constrains where a provider's keys may live. A third chain
is shorter and needs nothing not already deployed: the hosted gateway funds every
Web2 caller from one wallet though \code{openSession} takes the owner as its first
argument, and the capital and compute domains each hold their own copy of the
emission schedule with nothing checking that they agree.

We have read the Morpheus tree carefully. Several parts of it are better than
their reputation, and Section~\ref{sec:good} credits them. We have a commercial
interest in this market working, and Section~\ref{sec:limits} states where that
interest and yours diverge, because a document that does not say so is not worth
reading. Ten one-page dockets accompany this paper, LP-312 through LP-321, each
self-contained. This document is the argument that connects them.

\section{The deployed system}

The facts in this section are read from the working tree at
\code{MorpheusAIs/*} as of August 2026 and stated without comment; the comment
starts in Section~\ref{sec:good}.

\paragraph{Assets and domains.} MOR is an ERC-20 on Ethereum, bridged to
Arbitrum and Base as a LayerZero OFT. Morpheus operates no chain of its own.
Capital contracts live on Ethereum, where \code{Distribution} splits each
period's emission across deposit pools. The compute market is a single EIP-2535
diamond on Base mainnet at
\code{0x6aBE1d282f72B474E54527D93b979A4f64d3030a}, owned by a 5-of-9 Gnosis Safe
at \code{0x1FE04BC15Cf2c5A2d41a0b3a96725596676eBa1E}, with \code{SessionRouter},
\code{Marketplace}, \code{ModelRegistry}, \code{ProviderRegistry} and
\code{Delegation} facets.

\paragraph{The bid.} A bid is six fields
(\code{IBidStorage.sol:14--21}):

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Solidity]
struct Bid {
    address provider;
    bytes32 modelId;
    uint256 pricePerSecond;
    uint256 nonce;
    uint128 createdAt;
    uint128 deletedAt;
}
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
One of the six is economic. Bidding is permissionless inside a
\code{bidMinPricePerSecond} / \code{bidMaxPricePerSecond} band and costs a
\code{bidFee} (\code{Marketplace.sol:63--90}).

\paragraph{The session.} \code{openSession(address user\_, uint256 amount\_,
bool isDirectPaymentFromUser\_, bytes approvalEncoded\_, bytes signature\_)}
validates the caller against the owner under
\code{DELEGATION\_RULES\_SESSION} (\code{SessionRouter.sol:90}), pulls
\code{amount\_} of MOR from \code{user\_} (:104), and computes
\code{endsAt} from the stipend the stake purchases divided by the bid price.
Session identity is
$\mathsf{keccak256}(\text{user} \| \text{provider} \| \text{bidId} \|
\text{nonce})$ (:159). Provider revenue on close is
$(\text{sessionEnd} - \text{openedAt}) \times \text{pricePerSecond}$, where
\code{sessionEnd} is $\min(\code{closedAt},\code{endsAt})$
(\code{\_getProviderRewards}, :264). \code{MIN\_SESSION\_DURATION} is five
minutes and \code{SIGNATURE\_TTL} is ten (\code{SessionStorage.sol:34--35}).

\paragraph{The stipend.} \code{stakeToStipend(amount, timestamp)} returns
$\text{amount} \times \code{getComputeBalance}(t) / (100 \times
\code{totalMORSupply}(t))$ (:183). \code{getComputeBalance} reads the diamond's
own \code{Pool[]} array at \code{COMPUTE\_POOL\_INDEX = 3} and subtracts
\code{providersTotalClaimed}, the cumulative amount providers have drawn (:484).
That is the only place realised compute demand feeds back into how much session
time the market can sell.

\paragraph{Payment.} Neither Base payout is a mint. \code{\_claimForProvider}
clamps the claim to \code{provider.stake - provider.limitPeriodEarned} and then
executes \code{safeTransferFrom(fundingAccount, provider, amount)} (:383--397).
On the builder side, \code{BuilderSubnets.sol:335} does
\code{safeTransferFrom(treasury, staker, toClaim\_)}. Payment therefore depends
on a balance and an approval held by accounts outside the contract.

\paragraph{The node.} \code{proxy-router} is a Go daemon. \code{:3333} carries
morrpc, the peer-to-peer protocol between consumer and provider nodes;
\code{:8082} is the local administrative API and must not be exposed. A
provider's backend is configuration: \code{ApiAdapterFactory} is constructed from
\code{modelConfig.ApiType}, \code{ApiURL} and \code{ApiKey}
(\code{ai\_engine.go:64}), and the OpenAI adapter POSTs to whatever base URL it
was given with a bearer token, overwriting the requested model name with the
configured one (\code{openai.go:58,64,70}).

\paragraph{The hosted path.} \code{Morpheus-Marketplace-API} is a FastAPI
service at \code{api.mor.org} presenting an OpenAI-compatible surface, so Web2
callers never hold a wallet. It maintains its own session pool, its own
credit ledger, and its own wallet-link table for users who have proved an
address by SIWE.

\paragraph{Governance.} MRC authorises the allocation of emission to the compute
pool. Its repository's last commit is dated 2025-11-17.

\section{What the design gets right}
\label{sec:good}

A document that only enumerates gaps invites the reader to discount all of it,
and would deserve to be discounted. Four things in this tree are better than
what we usually find, and two of them are better than what we have built
ourselves.

\subsection{The attestation stack}

\code{proxy-router/internal/attestation} is 5{,}439 lines of Go and it is
careful work. The TDX \code{report\_data} is checked against the SHA-256 of the
SubjectPublicKeyInfo DER of the TLS certificate belonging to the peer the
verifier actually reached, which is the check that stops a valid quote from a
genuine enclave being replayed in front of a different endpoint
(\code{verifier.go:292}). The NVIDIA \code{eat\_nonce} is cross-checked against
the CPU quote and compared in constant time. Golden measurements are pinned to a
Sigstore identity, an OIDC issuer and an identity regexp naming the repository,
rather than to a hash in a config file (\code{golden.go:23--30}).

Two dependencies deserve to be named because they are load-bearing.
\code{VerifyQuote} POSTs the quote to a third party's parsing endpoint and infers
validity from the JSON that comes back (\code{verifier.go:563}); and the
measurement registry is fetched unsigned from a CDN copy of an npm package,
because the upstream repository was removed
(\code{artifacts\_registry.go:16--20}). The comment in that file is honest about
why. The engineering is sound and the root of trust underneath it is rented.

\subsection{The bounty}

The bug bounty is scoped by deployed contract address and requires a proof of
concept. That is a materially better design than a scope defined by repository
name, because it makes the deployed artefact the object of the claim rather than
the source tree, and because a mandatory PoC filters the class of report that
consumes reviewer time without producing information. We intend to submit
against it rather than around it, including for the transport finding in
Section~\ref{sec:rebind}.

\subsection{Treasury bifurcation, and the July 2026 incident}

On 2026-07-31 a stipend recycling defect was patched on Base mainnet.
\code{\_rewardUserAfterClose} had refunded a consumer's full stake on a late
close without anchoring the day-lock to when the session stopped consuming
compute, so the same MOR could be recycled inside a UTC day to draw repeated
stipends. The current code anchors on $\min(\code{closedAt},\code{endsAt})$ and
locks until the start of the following day, with the reason in a comment
naming the pull request.

The part worth crediting is not the fix, it is the containment. Because provider
payment runs \code{safeTransferFrom} from a funding account rather than minting,
and because the compute pool is bifurcated from the rest of the emission, the
exploitable ceiling was bounded at roughly the compute treasury's daily draw,
around one percent of the pool [verified], rather than at the token supply. A design where
the payment path cannot mint converted an unbounded economic bug into a bounded
one. That property was chosen before the bug existed.

\subsection{Receipt-derived accounting, and the delegation primitive}

Two smaller things. First, the hosted gateway derives day-locks from close
receipts read back from chain
(\code{session\_routing\_service.py:891}) rather than from its own optimistic
bookkeeping, which is the correct direction of trust for a component that cannot
correct the chain. Second, \code{\_isValidProviderReceipt} already accepts a
receipt signed by the provider, by a delegate holding
\code{DELEGATION\_RULES\_PROVIDER}, or by \code{owner()} when the provider
address is a contract (\code{SessionRouter.sol:537--559}). A keyless provider is
already a supported shape. Section~\ref{sec:custody} finishes it rather than
inventing it.

We also note the capacity manager interface is factored correctly.
\code{HasCapacity(modelID string) bool} is the whole thing
(\code{capacity\_manager.go:12}), with implementations selected by config
(:112). Adding admission policy is a case in a switch, not a refactor.

\section{The unit of account}
\label{sec:unit}

\subsection{Revenue is a function of time; cost is a function of choice}

Let $p$ be a bid's \code{pricePerSecond}, $T$ the settled session duration,
$\pi$ a provider's policy for choosing which backend answers each request,
$c(\pi)$ the expected backend cost per paid second under that policy, and
$Q(\pi)$ the delivered quality. Settlement pays $pT$. It does not read $\pi$,
cannot read $c$, and has no representation of $Q$. Provider profit is

\[
  \Pi(\pi) \;=\; \big(p - c(\pi)\big)\,T ,
\]

\noindent
which is maximised by minimising $c(\pi)$ subject to whatever floor the provider
chooses to honour. Nothing in the protocol supplies that floor.

This is not a criticism of per-second pricing. Per-token pricing has its own
pathologies, needs a tokeniser both sides agree on, and would have to be metered
by an oracle the network does not have. Per-second pricing is metered by
\code{block.timestamp}, which is the cheapest honest meter available on a chain
you do not operate. The point is narrower: the decision fixes where the margin
lives, and the margin lives entirely in model selection.

\subsection{Routing alpha, defined}

Define the gap between pinning one model and routing optimally at the same
quality floor $\bar q$:

\[
  \alpha(\bar q) \;=\; c(\pi_{\mathrm{pin}}) \;-\;
  \min_{\pi \,:\, Q(\pi) \ge \bar q} c(\pi).
\]

\noindent
$\alpha$ is bounded below by the mass of requests that a cheaper admissible
backend answers at or above the floor, multiplied by the price spread of the
admissible pool. It is non-negative by construction, since pinning is feasible.
It is non-increasing in $\bar q$, which is the fact Section~\ref{sec:dual} turns
on.

A learned router computes exactly the constrained minimisation on the right.
Hanzo's router, Enso, selects per request in microseconds on CPU, maximising
$Q(\pi) - \lambda c(\pi) - \mu \ell(\pi)$ over the set of backends that satisfy
the request's service objective, where $\ell$ is latency. On a per-second
market the multipliers are not free parameters. Normalising profit by $pT$
leaves $1 - c(\pi)/p$, so the cost weight scales as $\lambda \propto 1/p$: a
cheap bid should route harder. The latency weight has no source in settlement at
all. A slower backend serves fewer requests inside the same paid window and
therefore costs the provider less, so on this market $\mu$ must come from the
consumer's own scoring, not from the contract.

\paragraph{An illustrative magnitude, and only that.} The network publishes no
price, session-length or volume series in any of its 62 repositories, so what
follows is shape rather than measurement. Suppose the cheapest admissible
backend costs $0.2$ against a pinned model's $1.0$, and seven requests in ten
clear the floor on it. Routed cost is $0.7(0.2) + 0.3(1.0) = 0.44$, so
$\alpha = 0.56$ of pinned cost [illustrative]. If pinned service consumed sixty
percent of bid revenue, routing takes cost of service to twenty-six percent of
revenue [illustrative]. Those inputs are assumptions. The first useful thing anyone can do here is measure
them, which is what Section~\ref{sec:seq} proposes as a first step.

\subsection{Price discovery, not a trading strategy}

An arbitrage this size does not stay with whoever finds it, and we are not
proposing that it should. \code{postModelBid} is permissionless inside the price
band, so as routed providers enter, $p$ is competed down toward $c(\pi^\star)$
and the surplus moves to consumers, who buy duration and buy more of it as $p$
falls. Morpheus has no process today that discovers what a session-second is
worth. Routing is one.

The constraint that binds first is deliberate and is yours:
\code{\_claimForProvider} clamps a period's claims to
\code{provider.stake - provider.limitPeriodEarned}
(\code{SessionRouter.sol:383}). Revenue is bounded by stake, so the arbitrage
runs out of capital before it runs out of competition. That is a good property
and Section~\ref{sec:verify} depends on it.

\subsection{The same functional at a lower floor}
\label{sec:dual}

$\alpha(\bar q)$ is non-increasing in $\bar q$. A router that quietly serves
below its advertised floor earns strictly more than one that honours it, and the
difference is the fraud surplus. Routing alpha and substitution fraud are the
same functional evaluated at two different floors.

On chain they are indistinguishable, because the backend is configuration and
not code. A provider that changes one JSON field to point at a smaller model
keeps the entire cost difference, produces no on-chain inconsistency, and
requires no cooperation from anyone. The node's own documentation is candid
about the adjacent case: \code{providers/resale/overview.mdx} instructs a resale
provider to bid on somebody else's model without the \code{tee} tag, on the
grounds that it does not control the backend. That is honest guidance. It is not
a protocol.

The market's own selection statistic points the wrong way. \code{ScoreInput}
(\code{rating/interface.go:15}) carries provider and model statistics, price per
second, provider stake and minimum stake; the default scorer weights tokens per
second, time to first token, session duration, success ratio and a stake term,
then adjusts for price (\code{scorer\_default.go:21--29}). Quality of output is
not an argument to it. Dropping the floor weakly improves throughput, weakly
improves time to first token, and strictly improves the price the provider can
afford to post. The ranking function rewards substitution.

\subsection{Committing to an admissible set}
\label{sec:disclose}

The obvious response, forbid routing, is both unenforceable and wrong. Routing
is how a provider serves heterogeneous demand efficiently, and the network
already contains it. The alternative extreme, publish the pool, deletes routers
as a business, because the pool and the policy are the product. There is a
middle construction and it is standard.

A provider registers, before the request, a commitment
$C = (\textit{root}, \textit{floor}, \textit{class}, k)$ against its bid:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{root}: a Merkle root over salted leaves
$H(\textit{manifest}_i \| s_i)$, one leaf per servable model. A manifest fixes
weight digests or upstream model identity, version, context length,
quantisation and serving configuration. The salt keeps an unopened leaf opaque.
\item \textbf{floor}: a quality floor on a named public benchmark, claimed for
every leaf in the set.
\item \textbf{class}: the class of selection rule, not the rule.
Cheapest-above-floor, lowest-latency-above-floor and learned-score are
observably different; the parameters stay private.
\item $k$: the size of the set, published, because it bounds every privacy
claim that can be made about the scheme.
\end{itemize}

Each response carries an opening: the leaf, its Merkle path, and a signature
over chain id, diamond address, \code{sessionId}, \code{requestId} and the
response hash, which prevents a flattering opening being replayed or moved
between requests. Verification is one hash chain and one \code{secp256k1}
recovery, a few hundred bytes on a response already kilobytes wide.

\paragraph{What leaks.} Openings accumulate into the multiset of served leaves
and their frequencies, which is the realised policy over the prompts a given
customer sent. $k$ is the dial. At $k=4$ under a stable rule the policy is
public within a day; at $k=60$ it remains a distribution, though hiding among
sixty concedes that the floor is doing little work. Estimating the policy to
accuracy $\varepsilon$ costs on the order of $k/\varepsilon^{2}$ paid requests
per prompt class, and re-salting each epoch restarts that bill. We state this
rather than claiming the scheme is private, because a router operator is the
party most exposed by it and that operator is us.

\paragraph{Type the bid, not the model.} Routing is a property of a provider's
serving path, but \code{Model.tags} (\code{IModelStorage.sol:22}) is written by
the model owner. The node already reads those tags to decide whether to demand
an attestation from a provider's backend (\code{isTeeModel},
\code{proxy\_receiver.go:123}), which is a model-owner field gating a
provider-side check. Put \emph{fixed} against \emph{routed} on the bid, where
the provider owns it, and buyers filter on a field rather than on a convention.

\subsection{Why disclosure needs verification}
\label{sec:verify}

A commitment makes substitution decidable. It does not detect it. Nothing in
Section~\ref{sec:disclose} stops a provider from opening leaf $i$ and serving
something else, and nothing establishes that the floor or the rule class held.
Disclosure without a way to check it is a statement, and the market already has
statements.

Three questions get merged in discussions of this and should be separated.

\begin{description}
\item[Is the box genuine?] Morpheus answers this, and answers it well. That is
the attestation stack of Section~\ref{sec:good}, with the two rented
dependencies named there.
\item[Did inference happen at all?] Nothing answers this.
\item[Were these the advertised weights?] HideNSeek attempted it. Its last
commit is 2024-07-29 and \code{main.py} is zero bytes on disk.
\end{description}

\noindent
The proposal is three layers, cheapest first.

\paragraph{Commit.} Add \code{bytes32 modelCommitment} to \code{Bid} and to
\code{postModelBid}, emitted in \code{MarketplaceBidPosted}. One \code{SSTORE}
per bid, none per request. A zero commitment stays legal and pledges nothing,
which honestly describes every bid on the network today.

\paragraph{Bind.} The 64 bytes of TDX \code{report\_data} are fully allocated
by the existing TLS binding, so the layout must not change. Change what the GPU
nonce \emph{is} instead:
\[
  \texttt{gpuNonce} \;=\; \mathsf{SHA256}\big(\texttt{"mor-gpu-nonce-v1"}
  \,\|\, c \,\|\, \rho\big),
\]
with $c$ the model commitment and $\rho$ a fresh random value published beside
the quote. Freshness is preserved, the existing CPU-to-GPU nonce cross-check is
untouched, and the Intel-signed quote now transitively covers $c$. Per response,
the enclave's TLS key signs
$(\code{sessionId}, i, c, \mathsf{H}(x), \mathsf{H}(y))$ for request index $i$,
input $x$ and output $y$, and the Merkle root over those leaves rides in the
close-out receipt. Without a TEE the same statement degrades to a plain
\code{secp256k1} signature, which is weaker evidence but still adjudicable.

\paragraph{Probe.} Sample sessions with known-answer challenges and slash on
contradiction. Let $\gamma$ be what substitution saves as a fraction of epoch
revenue, $\lambda$ the fraction of stake slashed on a caught contradiction, and
$\beta$ the probability that a single probe catches a substituting provider.
Expected penalty exceeds expected saving when

\[
  m \;\ge\; \frac{1}{\beta}\,
  \ln\!\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda - \gamma}\right)
\]

\noindent
probes per provider per epoch. At $\gamma = 0.9$, $\lambda = 1$ and
$\beta = 0.3$ that is eight [illustrative]. Two features of the bound matter. It carries no
traffic term, so the probe duty cycle falls as $1/\text{volume}$ and the scheme
gets cheaper as the market grows. And it requires $\lambda > \gamma$: stake
smaller than the prize deters nothing at any probe rate. That condition is
already close to satisfiable here, because \code{\_claimForProvider} denominates
a period's earnings in the same units as \code{provider.stake}. The bond exists;
it is currently only a withdrawal delay.

\subsection{Why verification wants an execution environment you specify}
\label{sec:l1}

Per-audit verification is affordable anywhere. Per-request verification is a gas
question, and gas is the one thing a tenant does not set.

Verifying a FIPS 204 ML-DSA signature costs 50{,}000 to 150{,}000 gas as a
native precompile and upward of ten million in Solidity. The second figure does
not merely cost more, it does not fit in a block anyone will pay for. The same
holds for the lattice primitives that Section~\ref{sec:conf} argues receipts
will eventually need. A rented execution environment will not add a precompile
because one tenant's market needs a predicate cheap.

Renting Base was the right call and we want to say that plainly. It bought a
working market with settlement, staking, disputes and an upgrade path years
before a chain of Morpheus's own could have existed, and most protocols that
build a chain first never reach the point where the chain matters. The costs are
specific and worth naming rather than asserting in general:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A settlement rule enforced in blocks you do not produce.}
\code{SIGNATURE\_TTL} is ten minutes against a five-minute
\code{MIN\_SESSION\_DURATION}, and \code{\_rewardProviderAfterClose} withholds
provider funds on a dispute only when the close is early
(\code{SessionRouter.sol:279--289}). An inclusion delay comparable to a session
length turns an early close into a late one and deletes the consumer's remedy.
Whether that has ever happened is a measurement, and Section~\ref{sec:seq} says
who should take it.
\item \textbf{A fee asset you do not issue}, so a consumer needs a second asset
before opening a session and fee revenue leaves for another chain's fee market.
\item \textbf{A precompile set you cannot extend}, which is the binding one.
\end{itemize}

A sovereign chain changes four things. Protocol invariants become consensus
transactions: ``used stake cannot be recycled inside the same UTC day'' becomes
a validity condition every validator checks, rather than a branch spread across
facets sharing a storage layout. MOR becomes the gas asset. Validator identity
carries the session material, so address-to-key binding is read from state
rather than asserted in a frame, which retires the parallel \code{secp256k1} PKI
described in Section~\ref{sec:rebind}. And verification becomes a precompile,
which is the one that Section~\ref{sec:verify} rests on.

Lux's construction is three transactions against the upstream Lux P-chain and
then independence: \code{CreateChainTx} creates the chain and its genesis,
\code{AddValidatorTx} sets the genesis validator set, and
\code{ConvertNetworkTx} promotes the network from inherited security to its own
set under an on-chain staking contract
(\code{platformvm/txs/convert\_network\_tx.go}). Afterwards the L1 runs its own
primary network with networkID equal to its EVM chainID, one number for wallets,
indexers and key derivation, and its validators stop talking to Lux at runtime.
Sovereignty is not binary: a hybrid mode that restakes the parent while growing
its own set pays the security budget as that set grows rather than on day one.

We are not recommending this today. Section~\ref{sec:seq} places it last and
Section~\ref{sec:limits} names the condition under which it is a bad idea.

\subsection{The chain, restated}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Because} & \textbf{therefore} \\
\midrule

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
Each step is implementable without the next. LP-315 needs no contract change at
all. LP-316 needs a manifest field and a response field. LP-313 needs one facet
replacement. LP-314 needs a chain. The chain is real but each link stands on its
own, which is why the sequencing in Section~\ref{sec:seq} is not all-or-nothing.

\section{Confidentiality}
\label{sec:conf}

\subsection{The asymmetry in the transport}

Consumer prompts cross \code{:3333} as plain JSON. Provider responses are ECIES
encrypted. \code{SessionPromptRequest} takes the provider's public key as an
argument and never uses it to encrypt the payload
(\code{morrpcmessage/mor\_rpc.go:471}); \code{SessionPromptReq.Message} is a
\code{string}, not a ciphertext (\code{request.go:55--60}); and all four
\code{EncryptString} call sites in \code{proxy\_receiver.go} run
provider-to-consumer (:255, :335, :611, :654). The protected half is the half
that usually matters less.

We do not think this was a decision. It has the shape of an asymmetry that
appeared once and was never revisited, which is how most of them appear. It is
also the cheapest thing in this document to fix and the one with the shortest
argument in front of it.

\subsection{What survives the obvious fix}

Encrypting the request closes the immediate hole and leaves the structural one.
Every confidentiality primitive in the tree is \code{secp256k1}: ECIES payloads
in \code{lib/crypto.go}, morrpc frame authentication, ECDSA approvals in
\code{SessionRouter}. Worse, the ECIES key is the same wallet key handed to
\code{bind.NewKeyedTransactorWithChainID}
(\code{blockchainapi/service.go:1796}), loaded from
\code{WALLET\_PRIVATE\_KEY} or the OS keychain (\code{config.go:48}). Its public
value is therefore recoverable from any Base transaction the account has ever
broadcast.

An adversary with a cryptographically relevant quantum computer does not attack
recorded ciphertext under that arrangement. It lifts the static private key off
public chain data and reads every session it captured. The asset in an inference
market is the payload, and a payload does not become less sensitive with age, so
the relevant horizon for this property is the working life of the person who
typed the prompt rather than the life of a session.

Attestation does not reach this. The 5{,}439 lines establish what runs inside an
enclave. They establish nothing about the channel that reaches the enclave, and
a passive recorder on \code{:3333} never touches the enclave.

\paragraph{Mechanism.} Session establishment becomes hybrid: X25519 and
ML-KEM-768 combined through HKDF, so the session key survives the failure of
either primitive. Traffic keys derive only after the transcript is bound to the
on-chain session identity, that is to chain id, diamond address,
\code{sessionId}, \code{bidId}, provider and user, so a channel cannot be
transplanted between sessions. Approvals become ECDSA $\wedge$ ML-DSA-65, with
the lattice leg checked where the approval is already verified once, inside the
consumer's proxy-router.

Base has no ML-DSA precompile and we will not pretend otherwise. Settlement
stays classical until a chain underneath it has one, which is the honest version
of the argument in Section~\ref{sec:l1}: solvency has a short horizon and
confidentiality an unbounded one, so they do not need the same primitive at the
same time. Migration is suite-version negotiation rather than a fork, with the
suite identifier inside the transcript hash so a downgrade fails as a bad
signature rather than silently. Lux moved validator authentication onto a hybrid
classical and ML-DSA suite on a live mainnet with no downtime, which is the only
evidence we have that this shape survives contact with an operating network.

\subsection{Peer identity is asserted, not resolved}
\label{sec:rebind}

Binding the transcript to the funded session closes a second thing, and this one
is exploitable today without a quantum computer.

\code{proxy\_receiver.go:526--528} stores, on a session request, the mapping
\code{user:<addr>} $\rightarrow$ \code{req.Key}. The signature on that request is
verified with \code{req.Key} itself
(\code{controller\_morrpc.go:125}), so the message asserts an identity and is
checked against its own assertion. Any peer that can reach \code{:3333} can
therefore rebind the public key a provider associates with a victim's address.
The consumer side has the same shape at \code{proxy\_sender.go:288--295},
seventy lines below the on-chain resolution the same file already implements at
:233.

The fix does not require the handshake. \code{VerifySignatureAddr} already
exists (\code{mor\_rpc.go:625}) and is used on the ping path. Anchoring the
\code{session.request} signature check to \code{req.User}, and requiring
\code{req.Key} to recover to that address, is a node-side diff with no wire
change, no new dependency and no contract upgrade. A patched provider protects
its own users whatever the client runs. Under the full handshake the peer key
comes from the recovered signer and the \code{SessionOpened} event, and never
from the message.

We will open this under the existing bounty with a proof of concept rather than
publishing it further. Everything else in this section rests on that check.

\subsection{A router that learns from traffic it cannot read}
\label{sec:learn}

A learned router improves because it observes what happened after each choice.
Section~\ref{sec:conf} closes the prompt inside a post-quantum session and
Section~\ref{sec:verify} closes the inference inside an enclave. The learner and
the confidentiality argument want opposite things from the same bytes, and the
reconciliation is not rhetorical: it is a statement about where the feature
extractor runs and what its output reveals.

A ledger row is $(\phi, m, r, c, \ell)$: a bucketed feature vector
$\phi \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, the model chosen, a scalar quality score, cost and
latency, driving a policy toward $r - \lambda c - \mu \ell$. No prompt text, no
completion text and no embedding appears in any field. The quality score comes
from a judge model running inside the same enclave that terminated the session:
it reads plaintext and emits one number. Consumer feedback, accept or retry or
discard, is content-free by construction and is the stronger signal. Updates
ship as aggregated $\Delta W$ over a minimum batch, which makes each shipped
object a statement about traffic rather than about a request.

Extraction has to happen inside the confidentiality boundary or the property
never existed. Compute $\phi$ outside the enclave and the prompt has already
left. The extractor is therefore part of the attested workload and the ledger's
only writer, pinned in the same golden set the verifier already fetches. That is
not new machinery; it is the machinery of Section~\ref{sec:good} pointed at a
new binary.

\paragraph{What a feature vector still says.} Content-free is not leak-free.

\begin{itemize}
\item $\phi$ carries task class, language, length bucket, difficulty estimate
and tool markers. Anyone holding the ledger recovers a usage profile. Three
short legal questions and a long code generation at 04:00 is a profile, and that
is the price.
\item Membership inference is the sharp risk and it scales with $d$. A
1024-dimensional embedding row fingerprints its prompt; thirty-two coarse
buckets do not. Fix $d$ small, quantise every field, bar embeddings at the
schema level, and publish how many distinct prompts collide on a single row.
\item Inversion against the policy recovers the feature regions where reward ran
high, summed over traffic. It stops being aggregate the moment a policy row is
fit to one account, so per-tenant policies stay in the enclave, gradients are
clipped, and nothing ships below the batch floor.
\item We do not claim differential privacy. An honest claim names a mechanism, a
noise scale and an accountant, and a ledger that lives for years exhausts any
fixed $\varepsilon$. DP on the update is a separate piece of work with a budget,
not a word borrowed here.
\end{itemize}

Under the threat model of Section~\ref{sec:conf} the ledger is the long-lived
half, so harvest-now applies to storage at least as much as to the wire. Rows
are sealed under ML-KEM, signed under ML-DSA, and keyed by a rotating session
pseudonym rather than by a consumer address. A feature vector written today is
an artefact an adversary keeps.

\subsection{Custody follows confidentiality}
\label{sec:custody}

A Morpheus provider runs one \code{secp256k1} key for three jobs with three
different lifetimes and three different blast radii: it holds stake, it prices
bids, and it signs every morrpc frame and receipt on a TCP port that must be
reachable from the open internet. A traffic key should be minutes old and
disposable. A stake key should never sit on a host that accepts inbound
connections. Once Section~\ref{sec:rebind} makes the traffic key the thing an
attacker on \code{:3333} is actually attacking, the conflation stops being
theoretical.

The indirection is built. \code{\_isValidProviderReceipt} already accepts a
delegate or an \code{owner()}, and \code{proxy-router} mirrors it:
\code{OnChainProviderAuthResolver} calls \code{owner()} on the consumer's own
RPC, keyed by the address the consumer chose, so it cannot be forged in transit.

The proposal is to finish it. The provider address becomes a custody contract
whose \code{owner()} is a threshold key in a KMS, offline from every node, and
stake authority lives there and nowhere else. Bid authority delegates
separately, since \code{DELEGATION\_RULES\_MARKETPLACE} already gates
\code{postModelBid} and \code{deleteModelBid}
(\code{Marketplace.sol:68,119}), so a warm key that can reprice but cannot
unstake is one delegation away.

Traffic authority is the right that does not exist yet, and its absence has a
concrete cost. \code{\_isValidProviderReceipt} accepts
\code{DELEGATION\_RULES\_PROVIDER}, which is the same right that gates
\code{providerRegister} and \code{providerDeregister}
(\code{ProviderRegistry.sol:30,62}). Letting a datacenter sign receipts
therefore also lets it deregister you. That is forced delisting rather than
theft, because withdrawal still addresses the provider, but it is the wrong
radius for a key on a box with \code{:3333} open. Split it and rotation stops
meaning re-registration: rotating or revoking a traffic key becomes a delegation
write, leaving address, endpoint, bids and stake untouched, and consumers pick
up the new signer on their next resolve.

Custody keys should be $t$-of-$n$ rather than a file. Lux's threshold signing
rotates holders without changing the public key, over two kernels: a
\code{secp256k1} kernel, which is what Base requires since \code{ECDSA.recover}
is the diamond's only verifier, and Pulsar, a lattice kernel whose combined
output is byte-equal to a single-party FIPS 204 ML-DSA-65 signature. Pulsar is
review-ready and not production-certified, and it does not verify on Base. It is
the path for custody that must outlive \code{secp256k1}, not a claim about
today. The \code{secp256k1} kernel buys the property that matters now: no
machine holds a whole key.

\paragraph{An unrelated defect in the same path.} MyProvider is the entire
supply-side onboarding flow, and its port check reads a \code{TypeError} from
\code{fetch} as proof that the port is open
(\code{apiService.ts:470--477}). A fetch to a closed port fails in exactly that
way, and \code{:3333} speaks morrpc rather than HTTP, so no browser can perform
this check at all. Operators stake MOR to advertise endpoints that nothing can
reach. The fix is a server-side probe completing one morrpc handshake, and it is
a day of work.

\section{Two repairs available with deployed primitives}

Neither item in this section needs a new primitive, a new chain, or a
cryptographic argument. Both are visible in code that shipped.

\subsection{Delegated sessions}
\label{sec:deleg}

The hosted gateway multiplexes arbitrarily many Web2 callers onto sessions
funded by one consumer wallet. The engineering around that pool is careful:
idle-session claiming under \code{FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED}, a cheapest-bid walk
with per-provider cool-off, spend fuses that ship inert and arm per environment,
day-locks derived from close receipts. Better fuses do not reach what is left.
One balance is a liveness dependency for every caller simultaneously; per-caller
accounting lives in a credit ledger the chain cannot correct, while the routed
session table carries no user id; and no caller has any claim on the session
serving them. The address that staked, that gets refunded, and that
\code{getUserSessions} returns is the gateway's.

Nothing on chain has to change for this to stop.
\code{openSession(address user\_, \dots)} validates the caller against
\code{user\_} under \code{DELEGATION\_RULES\_SESSION} at
\code{SessionRouter.sol:90} and then pulls stake from \code{user\_} at :104.
Caller and owner are already separate variables, and
\code{SessionRouter.test.ts:208} already tests opening a session from a
delegatee address. One argument in the Go node forecloses the whole
construction:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Go]
// registries/session_router.go:61 and :78
sessionTx, err := g.sessionRouter.OpenSession(opts, opts.From, stake,
                                              directPayment, approval, approvalSig)
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
\code{opts.From} is the node's own address, fed by \code{OpenSessionByBidId}
reading \code{s.GetMyAddress(ctx)} (\code{blockchainapi/service.go:1259}). The
session owner is hard-wired to the caller.

Under delegation, the gateway holds a right instead of custody. The consumer
writes \code{delegateContract(gateway, diamond, DELEGATION\_RULES\_SESSION,
true)} to the registry directly; the gateway opens with \code{user\_} set to
that consumer. Stake leaves the consumer's balance and every exit is addressed
back to them, \code{\_rewardUserAfterClose} to \code{session.user} and
\code{withdrawUserStakes} to \code{user\_}. That is the loss bound: pooled, a
compromised gateway key yields the pool; delegated, the worst it does is burn a
stipend claim and day-lock that one consumer's principal for under a day,
because the contract offers it no other address.

Two caps already hold in deployed code and revoke without the gateway's
cooperation: the consumer's ERC-20 allowance to the diamond, and
\code{maxSessionDuration}. What a boolean \code{checkDelegateForContract}
conveys should be said plainly, which is nothing else: no per-open size limit,
no expiry, no model restriction. The registry holds the first of those already,
since \code{delegateERC20(to, MOR, rights, amount)} publishes an upper bound
readable through \code{checkDelegateForERC20}. Expiry and model scope want a
small facet, and that is the second move, not the first.

\paragraph{The key problem, head on.} A delegation needs a consumer key, and
account-native users do not have one. That cost does not disappear. But the
gateway already knows which callers do: \code{wallet\_links} stores addresses
proved by SIWE, and that cohort moves with no new onboarding at all. For
everyone else the pool stays, much smaller, with a sponsored delegation from a
smart account as the route off it.

\subsection{Anchoring the compute domain to the capital domain}
\label{sec:anchor}

Capital lives on Ethereum. Compute lives on Base. The Base diamond holds its own
copy of the emission schedule, a \code{Pool[]} array written by
\code{setPoolConfig} under \code{onlyOwner}, whose \code{@dev} comment says the
parameters ``should be the same as in Ethereum L1 Distribution contract'' at
\code{0x47176B2Af9885dC6C4575d4eFd63895f7Aaa4790}, and instructs the reader to
call \code{Distribution.pools(3)} to check
(\code{SessionRouter.sol:58--60}). Nothing does. MOR crosses as a LayerZero OFT
and a single Python service sits between the two economies.

The coupling variable is \code{getComputeBalance}, cumulative compute emission
minus \code{providersTotalClaimed}. Through \code{stakeToStipend} it determines
how much session time a given stake purchases, which makes it the only channel
by which realised demand feeds back into what the market can sell. Ethereum
cannot read it.

Both domains call the same deployed \code{LinearDistributionIntervalDecrease}
with independently configured arguments. A relative error $\beta$ in the
decrease rate $d$ produces a relative error of

\[
  \frac{\beta d n}{R_0 - d n}
\]

\noindent
in the remaining pool at day $n$, where $R_0$ is the initial reward. This
diverges as $R_0 - dn \to 0$: the mirror is least reliable exactly when the pool
is smallest and the numbers matter most.

\paragraph{What must cross, and what must not.} Minimising the crossed state is
the design, so it should be bounded explicitly. Four scalars and two commitments
per UTC day: \code{providersTotalClaimed}, settled session-seconds, sessions
closed, and $\min(\text{balance}, \text{allowance})$ of the funding account,
which is the real cap on payable capacity; plus a Merkle root over per-provider
claim totals and a digest of the pool config in force. Excluded: sessions, bids,
prices, prompts, model identity, telemetry. A daily digest of fixed size is the
right object. A message bus is not.

\paragraph{Three levels of verification, with the adversary named.} The
adversary is the diamond owner together with the funding-account operator, who
can rewrite the pool config, repoint \code{fundingAccount}, or simply stop
funding, all silently.

\begin{description}
\item[Attested digest with a challenge window.] Nearly free. Guarantees only
that a false digest cannot pass unopposed.
\item[Storage proof against a finalised Base output root.] Removes the trusted
party, using machinery Ethereum tooling already ships, at the price of the
rollup's fault window.
\item[A native quorum certificate.] Removes that window and requires a compute
domain that produces one. Lux cross-chain finality applies between Lux chains,
not into a Base diamond. If this is the goal, it is Section~\ref{sec:l1}'s
problem, not this section's.
\end{description}

\paragraph{The cheap intermediate.} Nothing above needs Lux, and the first step
needs nothing from anyone outside Morpheus. Three changes to Base and Ethereum
as they stand: a \code{view} returning \code{keccak256} of the pool config each
side holds, so anyone can diff them; \code{SessionClosed}
(\code{ISessionRouter.sol:10}) widened to carry duration and settled amount, so
demand is reconstructible from logs alone; and balance and allowance exposed
alongside the daily budget. The first of those is an afternoon. Either the
mirror holds, or the divergence is a number.

This matters because MRC authorises the compute allocation, and the figure that
would tell MRC whether that allocation tracks real demand is
\code{providersTotalClaimed}, sitting in Base storage MRC cannot see. Anchoring
does not fix a governance process. It gives it a number to check.

\section{Sessions as an agent substrate}
\label{sec:agents}

This section is the one place where the programme is about new revenue rather
than repair.

Per-second billing is a poor fit for chat, where the session is mostly a human
reading. It is close to the right fit for an agent trajectory, where the gaps
are the agent working. Model a trajectory as alternating phases
$(g_1, t_1, \dots, g_n, t_n)$, with $g_i$ seconds generating at $\bar r$ tokens
per second and $t_i$ seconds blocked on a tool. Write $G = \sum g_i$,
$T = G + \sum t_i$ and $\rho = G/T$. A per-token price $\kappa$ charges
$\kappa \bar r G$ and ignores every $t_i$. A per-second bid $p$ charges $pT$, an
effective $p/(\bar r \rho)$ per token. They agree at

\[
  \rho^{\star} \;=\; \frac{p}{\kappa \bar r}.
\]

\noindent
Chat sits far below $\rho^\star$. An agent bursting against local tools sits
near $\rho = 1$. Per-second is worse exactly where $\rho$ collapses: forty
seconds blocked on a slow external API buys forty seconds of GPU price and no
work. The unit is right for one workload and wrong for another, and knowing
which is which is a measurement nobody has published.

It is an admission problem before it is a pricing problem, and admission is
factored well already. \code{HasCapacity(modelID)} is the entire interface;
\code{simple} counts open sessions, \code{idle\_timeout} counts sessions active
inside a hardcoded fifteen minutes (\code{capacity\_manager.go:72}). That is the
right instinct at the wrong resolution. \code{PromptActivity} already carries
\code{SessionID}, \code{StartTime} and \code{EndTime}
(\code{storages/structs.go:32}), which is enough to seat a second consumer in
the slot a parked one leaves idle. It is one more case in
\code{CreateCapacityManager} (:112).

Two things a trajectory needs that a session is not.

\paragraph{Tools run on the provider.} \code{CallAgentTool(ctx, sessionID,
agentID, toolName, input)} (\code{ai\_engine.go:195}) runs a registered agent's
configured command as an MCP stdio child of the provider process. The provider
executes the tool on its own credentials, is paid per second for as long as it
takes, and the consumer authorises this by holding a session without seeing what
ran. A provider running slow tools is paid more. Admission and settlement are
the same question here, which is why the capacity work is a precondition rather
than an optimisation.

\paragraph{A session spans neither a second bid nor its own funding.}
Conversation history keys on \code{chat\_id}, a client header randomised when
absent (\code{controller\_http.go:217}), while tools and payment key on
\code{sessionId}. A trajectory that wants two models therefore holds two
sessions whose tool state cannot join even though their \code{chat\_id} does.
And \code{endsAt} is fixed at open with no extend, so outrunning the funding
means resuming under a new \code{sessionId} with history intact and tool handles
gone.

One client-side object closes both: a \code{chat\_id} plus a set of live
\code{sessionId}s, opened before the current one expires rather than after, one
per bid, presenting a single tool namespace across them. Because latency under a
per-second bid \emph{is} cost, the policy falls out of the arithmetic: hold the
cheap session open across waits, and spend into the expensive one only while
generating.

The current agent story on the network is seven repositories. Five are
Vercel-derived templates that set an OpenAI base URL at \code{api.mor.org} and
never touch a session; \code{sim-studio-morpheus} and \code{Perplexica-morpheus}
carry the name and little else. The marketplace bills in the unit agents
consume and has no agent runtime. That is an opportunity rather than an
indictment.

\subsection{What the usage is worth}

State the mechanism plainly. A fee on verified volume feeds the existing buyback.
Revenue then scales with inference sold rather than with deposits.

Today the buyback runs on deposit yield, about \$511K over the trailing year,
and that figure rises and falls with interest rates, not with usage [verified].
Put a fee on verified sessions instead. Assume a fee equivalent to \$0.05 per
verified session [illustrative]. The low case, 10{,}000 sessions a day, is 3.65M
sessions a year, about \$183K [illustrative]; the high case at the same fee is
about \$1.83M [illustrative].

The size is not the argument. The derivative is. Yield revenue falls as rates
fall. Usage revenue rises as sessions rise. A fee on verified volume moves the
buyback onto the axis the emission is meant to reward, and it makes the receipt
in \code{mord} the thing revenue depends on, which is why the receipt has to be
un-fakeable before the fee means anything.

\subsection{Sequence, and why us}

Demand and verifiability first: routing that produces real sessions, and a
receipt that settles them honestly. Token rails second: governance with a
timelock, the LX order book, \code{lux/liquid}, \code{lux/bank}. An L2 and a
raise third. A raise clears only into demonstrated on-chain revenue and a KYC
base already onboarded through Hanzo IAM and \code{lux/wallet}, not into a
projection.

\paragraph{Relevance.} Morpheus needs two things it cannot build from inside its
own tree: buyers, and an un-fakeable receipt. Hanzo has the buyers, a launch
reach above $10^8$ [client], and the router. Lux has the settlement primitive,
\code{mord}, built [verified], and the chain that can price its verification in
gas. No other counterparty brings both the demand and the proof to the same
market. That is the case for this specific fix, made to this specific network.

\subsection{What could ship in a quarter}

Five items, none of which requires a 5-of-9 signature.

\begin{stake}{Work Hanzo does, and publishes whatever else happens}
\item Register a provider on the Base diamond, bid on an existing
\code{modelId}, serve it from our endpoint with the router choosing the backend,
and publish $\alpha$ measured against a pinned control over the same traffic.
Bidding is permissionless inside the price band, so this needs no facet upgrade
and no cooperation.
\item Write the third capacity manager against \code{dev} and publish $\rho$,
generating seconds against paid seconds, over that provider's own traffic. Both
figures are absent from all 62 repositories, and both are the network's whether
or not anything else here is adopted.
\item Thread an optional session owner through \code{OpenSessionByBidId} into
\code{session\_router.go}, defaulting to \code{opts.From} so existing
deployments behave identically. The contract tests already exist.
\item Publish the routing ledger schema with $d$ fixed and the extractor as a
reproducible build, and submit its measurement for pinning in the golden set the
verifier already trusts.
\end{stake}

\begin{stake}{Work a Morpheus contributor does, mostly in an afternoon each}
\item Land the \code{session.request} signature anchor: check against
\code{req.User}, require \code{req.Key} to recover to it. One node-side diff. We
will file it through the bounty with a PoC first.
\item Add the pool-config digest view to the diamond, read
\code{Distribution.pools(3)} on Ethereum, and compare. No counterparty, no
upgrade beyond a facet.
\item Export \code{SessionOpened} and \code{SessionClosed} and join them against
Base inclusion history to compute the fraction of closes pushed past
\code{endsAt}. That single number decides whether the liveness argument in
Section~\ref{sec:l1} is real or theoretical, and it is a day of work.
\item Replace the MyProvider port check with a server-side morrpc handshake
probe. This is unglamorous and it is currently the first thing every new
provider encounters.
\end{stake}

\subsection{What is a year}

LP-313 is a year in the sense that its three layers are three separate pieces of
work with different owners. The commitment field is a facet replacement and a
week. The GPU nonce binding is a change to an attested workload plus a verifier
update, which means a release cycle and an audit. The probe and slashing layer
is a new economic mechanism with a governance dependency, and mechanisms with
governance dependencies do not ship in a quarter on any network.

LP-314 is a year and should not start until LP-313's roadmap is real. A chain is
justified by a predicate that must run per request; without that predicate the
case reduces to fee capture, and fee capture does not close until roughly
$10^5$ sessions per day. We say that as the party that would benefit from
Morpheus running on Lux.

LP-318's custody contract and KMS adapter are a quarter of our work and a
one-constant change of yours, but the useful version of it waits for LP-312's
transport, because splitting traffic authority matters most once the traffic key
is the thing being attacked.

\subsection{Staffing, realistically}

Native output on this org is roughly 100 to 500 commits a month from about five
people, and some of what looks like native work is agent-written. That is the
constraint every proposal here has to survive.

What it implies: the Morpheus-side work in Section~\ref{sec:seq} totals about
four engineer-weeks, deliberately, and none of it is on the critical path of
anything else the team is doing. The larger items are ours to staff. Where a
proposal needs a facet replacement we have said so explicitly and kept it to one
transaction under the existing Safe, because a five-person team's scarcest
resource is not engineering time, it is the number of upgrade transactions it
can review properly in a quarter.

We would rather land four small things that are obviously correct than one large
thing that needs a governance vote.

\section{Limits}
\label{sec:limits}

\subsection{What no one in this programme can fix}

Nothing here proves that the arithmetic of an inference was right. An enclave
that loads the pledged weights and then truncates the attention window produces
a valid receipt under every mechanism in Section~\ref{sec:verify}. Proving
inference in zero knowledge is out of reach at these model sizes and we are not
going to imply otherwise.

Nothing here constrains what a provider does with plaintext it necessarily
holds. A sound channel delivers the prompt to a backend the provider
configured. Attestation narrows this to what a measured binary does, which is
narrower than trust and wider than nothing.

Nothing here creates demand. A better-verified market with no users is a
better-verified empty market, and every mechanism in this document is a cost
until volume exists to amortise it.

A compromised diamond owner can rewrite state before a digest is taken, so
Section~\ref{sec:anchor} transports the compute domain's accounting faithfully
without making it true.

And per-second pricing, which is the premise of the whole first chain, is not
something we propose to change. Everything in Section~\ref{sec:unit} is
downstream of a decision we think was correct.

\subsection{What depends on data the network does not publish}

Three quantities in this document are assumptions and are marked as such
wherever they appear.

$\alpha$ is unmeasured. The illustrative figures in Section~\ref{sec:unit} are
shape, not measurement, because no price, volume or session-length series exists
in any of the 62 repositories. If the real spread between admissible backends is
small, LP-315 is a smaller idea than it looks and LP-316 loses most of its
motivation.

$\rho$ is unmeasured. If agent traffic on this network turns out to be
tool-blocked most of the time, per-second pricing is worse for agents than the
argument in Section~\ref{sec:agents} implies, and the honest conclusion would be
that sessions want a suspend primitive rather than that agents want sessions.

The fraction of closes pushed past \code{endsAt} is unmeasured, and it is the
whole empirical content of the liveness argument against renting Base. If that
fraction is zero over a year of history, the strongest concrete cost of tenancy
disappears and the case for LP-314 rests on precompiles alone.

We have proposed the measurements rather than the conclusions in all three
cases, and where a first step is ours we will publish the number whether or not
it supports the proposal attached to it.

\subsection{Where our interests and yours diverge}

Four places, and naming them is the point of this section.

\paragraph{We benefit from routing being legitimate.} Hanzo operates a learned
router. Sections~\ref{sec:unit} and~\ref{sec:disclose} argue for a regime in
which routing is disclosed and permitted rather than prohibited, which is the
regime in which our product is sellable on this network. A network that decided
instead to require pinned models and verify them would be a worse fit for us and
might be better for consumers. We think the disclosure construction is the right
answer on the merits, and you should discount our confidence accordingly. The
partial defence is that LP-316 constrains us more than anyone: $k$, a floor and
a rule class are things we would have to publish, and no competing router
publishes them today.

\paragraph{We benefit from Morpheus running on Lux.} Section~\ref{sec:l1} is a
proposal to move settlement onto infrastructure we operate. We have tried to
write it as a decision with thresholds rather than a recommendation, we have put
it last in the sequence, we have said that fee capture alone does not justify it,
and we have named a team that cannot staff chain operations as a veto rather
than an obstacle. It is still the item in this programme where our interest is
most obviously served, and it is the one to read most sceptically.

\paragraph{We want traffic diversity, and that is what LP-319 buys us.} A
content-free ledger fed by a live per-second market is training signal we cannot
generate from our own endpoint. Section~\ref{sec:learn} is written to make the
leak surface auditable precisely because we are asking for something. The
schema, the field list and the extractor build are the concessions; they exist
because we are the beneficiary.

\paragraph{We are a competitor in one line of business.} Hanzo sells inference
through an OpenAI-compatible endpoint. So does \code{api.mor.org}. If this
programme succeeds, the hosted Morpheus gateway and our endpoint compete for
some of the same buyers, and we would be selling into a market whose verification
and settlement layer we helped design. We do not think that is disqualifying, but
it is a reason to insist that every mechanism here be specified well enough for
someone else to implement without us. Each one-pager says so in the same words:
implementable without adopting Lux or Hanzo software. That sentence is a
commitment, and it is the one we would most like to be held to.
