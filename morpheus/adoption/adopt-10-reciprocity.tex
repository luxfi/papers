\vspace{0.9ex}

\noindent\textbf{The number.}
\$8.9k. That is Morpheus's trailing 24-hour MOR volume, on a \$17M market cap \prov{verified}. MOR
trades at \$1.95 against \$1.4M of DEX depth, 88.8\% of it protocol-owned and held in one Safe
\prov{verified}. MOR has one live use, and that use is farming emissions. Emission runs 14,400 MOR a
day, roughly ten to one over real revenue \prov{verified}. No outside party stakes MOR to buy
anything.

\vspace{0.35ex}
\noindent\textbf{The problem, in their numbers.}
The rest of this series moves value one way. A venue takes MOR's liquidity (ADOPT-06), a lender its
collateral (ADOPT-04), a bank its balances (ADOPT-05), a router its settlement (ADOPT-07). Each is
worth adopting. Each, read alone, carries value toward Lux, Hanzo, or Zoo. A partner weighing them
fairly asks what comes back. With no return path, a good integration reads as extraction. And MOR
still has no use that is neither farming nor speculation.

\vspace{0.35ex}
\noindent\textbf{The fix, named to a repo.}
Morpheus already built the return path. A builder subnet is a staking-pool struct: \code{name},
\code{owner}, \code{minStake}, \code{fee}, \code{feeTreasury}, and the stake timing
(\code{IBuilderSubnets.sol:21}). \code{createSubnet} registers one and is open to any address
(\code{BuilderSubnets.sol:167}); \code{stake} directs MOR toward it (\code{:254}); \code{claim} pays
the staker its share of builder emissions net of a fee the owner sets and can only lower
(\code{:308}, \code{:225}). It lives in \code{SmartContracts} on Arbitrum One (chain 42161) and
Base, not the Base diamond, so registering is a call any address makes, not a governance action: no
facet upgrade, no 5-of-9 Safe signature \prov{verified}. Hanzo, Lux, and Zoo each call
\code{createSubnet}. MOR stakes toward our work to buy access to our surfaces, and we may take the
fee on those claims. Morpheus registers the mirror on our side: a MOR pool on \code{lux/dex} ``LX'',
a placement in Hanzo's router and app, an allocation in Zoo's research network. Same object, same
call, pointed back. No new code on either side.

\vspace{0.35ex}
\noindent\textbf{The outcome, projected.}
Assumptions stated. The cost is six transactions, three each side, and zero new lines
\prov{verified}. This alone adds no revenue and prices no MOR: a subnet routes stake, not demand
\prov{verified}. Its value is what it makes cheap. Best case: the three subnets are the on-ramp the
rest of the series composes onto. Assume ADOPT-07's funnel lands, 100M launch reach \prov{client},
2\% turning monthly active, 2\% of those into a private-inference tier, half routed
\prov{illustrative}; those users stake MOR toward three named builders for access, and a fee on
verified volume feeds the buyback Morpheus already runs, about \$511K over the trailing year
\prov{verified}. Then MOR gains a use that scales with usage, not deposits. Absent that funnel, the
loop is still a live MOR-denominated relationship with three surfaces carrying 100M+ reach
\prov{client}, for the price of six transactions.

\vspace{0.35ex}
\noindent\textbf{Relevance, and first step.}
No other counterparty can return a symmetric leg: a MOR pool, a router placement, and a research
allocation across three live networks, for the cost of three transactions. The mirror exists only
because both sides already run the same staking mechanism. It adopts today. Each of Hanzo, Lux, and
Zoo calls \code{createSubnet} with a name, an owner, a \code{minStake}, and a fee it chooses;
Morpheus registers the return leg in the same window. Subnets show on the Builders dashboard the day
they land, and \code{active.mor.org} carries the live state. Demand and verifiability land first,
token rails second, an L2 and a raise third.
