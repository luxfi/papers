\proposalhead{Self-Repaying Lending Against MOR}{%
About \$20M of stETH sits in Morpheus and pays the depositor who supplied it nothing they can
spend. \code{lux/liquid} makes that stETH borrowable without a sale and lists MOR as collateral for
the first time.}{ADOPT-04}

\noindent\textbf{The deposit pays the treasury, not the depositor.}
Third-party depositors supply roughly \$20M of stETH to the Morpheus capital pool \prov{verified}.
The yield, on the order of 5\%, routes to Aave v3 through \code{DistributorV2.sol}
(\code{@aave/core-v3 IPool}, line~10) \prov{verified} and accrues to the protocol treasury. Seventy-five
percent of that treasury flow funds a buyback, about \$511K per year \prov{verified}. The depositor is
left holding a claim on MOR emissions and stETH they cannot spend without unwinding the position.
MOR does about as little. It farms emissions or it trades; market cap is about \$17M and its
collateral use is zero \prov{verified}. Neither the collateral nor the token can be borrowed against
without giving one up.

\vspace{0.35ex}
\noindent\textbf{The fix: \code{lux/liquid}.}
\code{lux/liquid} (MIT) is a self-repaying lending engine on Lux. Deposit a yield-bearing asset into
\code{VaultV2} and mint a transferable 1:1 synthetic against it, up to the position's
collateralization floor \prov{verified repo}. The collateral keeps yielding; that yield is harvested
and burned against the debt, so the balance owed falls on its own with no repayment to schedule.
Capital does not sit in one venue. It routes through about a dozen risk-capped adapters, among them
Aave, Compound, Lido, EtherFi, Pendle, Morpho, Yearn, Ethena, EigenLayer, Maker DSR, and native Lux
strategies \prov{verified repo}. One counterparty's failure is bounded by its cap, not by the whole
book. List MOR as a supported deposit and it becomes borrowable collateral.

\vspace{0.35ex}
\noindent\textbf{The outcome, quantified.}
Take the \$20M of stETH already deposited \prov{verified} at a 50\% collateralization floor, standard
for yield-bearing collateral \prov{illustrative}. That is about \$10M of spendable liquidity minted
against deposits that produce \$0 spendable for the depositor today \prov{illustrative}, and the
depositor keeps the staking yield and the MOR emissions. MOR gains its first non-speculative demand:
listed at an initial cap of \$2M \prov{illustrative}, it moves from zero collateral use to
borrowable, and the demand is sourced from lending rather than from the emission schedule. Yield
diversifies from Aave-only near 5\% \prov{verified} across a dozen capped adapters, each classified
and allocated under governance.

\begin{stake}{What Morpheus gets}
  \item Its stETH becomes spendable without a sale, and MOR becomes borrowable collateral for the
        first time. Nothing in the Morpheus capital contracts has to change to list it.
  \item A worked, MIT-licensed engine whose yield is spread across a dozen capped adapters instead
        of one, so treasury income no longer rides a single counterparty.
\end{stake}

\noindent\textbf{Relevance, and first step.}
One market on Lux lists stETH, self-repaying today, and MOR, borrowable today. \code{lux/liquid} is
built and it is the lending leg of a set: it pairs with the bank that custodies the collateral
(ADOPT-05) and the DEX that prices the synthetic and MOR against it (ADOPT-06). Lux is the
counterparty that already runs all three, so the depositor never leaves one book to use the next.

\vspace{0.35ex}
\noindent\textbf{What this does not solve.}
Self-repayment is a property of yield-bearing collateral. MOR listed as bare collateral is
borrowable, but a MOR position only retires itself if MOR is put to work in a yield strategy of its
own. The synthetic holds its peg through the redemption schedule and collateral quality, not by
decree.
