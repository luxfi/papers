\title{LP-187: Physical Entropy for Lux\\Where It Belongs, Where It Does Not,
and How to Build a Source You Can Justify}
\author{Lux Industries, Inc.}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Lux draws every secret it holds --- validator keys, ML-DSA and ML-KEM key
pairs, TLS material, signing nonces, threshold dealer seeds --- from some
source, and each is exactly as unpredictable as that draw. This paper specifies
a physical entropy source for those draws, and argues the boundary that keeps
it out of consensus. The boundary is the substantive claim: a hardware source
improves secrets and would damage sortition, and the reason is not a matter of
quality but of kind. We give the construction that composes sources so that
adding one can never weaken the result, the health tests without which a source
is a noise generator rather than an entropy source, and open reference designs
at three price points, the cheapest of which is a diode and an ADC. We are
explicit about what such a device does not establish: entropy delivered over a
network is not verifiable by its recipient, and no measurement of a byte string
reveals how it was produced.
\end{abstract}

\section{Two problems that look alike}

Randomness appears in a blockchain in two places that share a word and share
nothing else.

The first is secrets. A validator's staking key must be unpredictable to
everyone but its holder. So must a signing nonce, a session key, and the dealer
seed from which a threshold ceremony expands its pairwise material. The
requirement is unpredictability to others, and it is satisfied by any process
the adversary cannot reproduce.

The second is agreement. When a protocol samples a committee, every honest node
must arrive at the same committee, and no participant may influence which one.
The requirement here is not unpredictability alone but \emph{unpredictability
together with public reproducibility}, which is a strictly harder thing.

A physical noise source answers the first requirement completely and the second
not at all. Its output is known to exactly one party, which is precisely what a
secret needs and precisely what agreement forbids.

\begin{claim}
A value that only one participant can produce is a value that participant can
produce repeatedly. Any protocol that lets such a value influence a public
outcome lets that participant choose among outcomes.
\end{claim}

This is why the boundary in \S\ref{sec:boundary} is not a limitation we accept
reluctantly. It is the reason the hardware is safe to deploy at all.

\section{The boundary}\label{sec:boundary}

Lux samples Pulsar committees from a seed fixed before the current block
exists:
\[
\mathrm{seed} = H\bigl(\text{``PULSAR\_SORTITION\_V1''} \parallel
B_{\mathrm{prev}} \parallel S \parallel h_P \parallel e \parallel \pi \bigr)
\]
where $B_{\mathrm{prev}}$ is the previously finalised block, $S$ the pinned
signer set, $h_P$ the P-Chain height, $e$ the epoch and $\pi$ the finality
posture. Every term was determined before the proposer began work. The seed is
therefore public, identical for every verifier, and beyond the proposer's
influence.

Substituting a locally drawn value inverts all three properties. The proposer
alone knows it; verifiers cannot recompute it; and nothing prevents redrawing
until the resulting committee is favourable. The substitution reads like an
upgrade --- physical randomness replacing a hash --- and is a downgrade of the
only property that mattered.

We state the rule plainly.

\begin{definition}[Entropy boundary]
A local entropy source may produce values that only its holder will ever know.
It may not produce, or contribute unaggregated to, any value another party must
independently reproduce.
\end{definition}

Public randomness is a separate construction. Where consumers genuinely need a
fresh public value --- a lottery, an auction ordering, a governance draw --- it
is produced by threshold signature (LP-176): unpredictable before it exists
because no minority can compute it, and verifiable afterwards because anyone
can check the signature. A participant may seed \emph{its own contribution}
from hardware. That is the single point at which the two layers meet, and it is
safe because the output is not any one contribution.

\section{What an entropy source is}

A noise source is not an entropy source. The distinction is the difference
between a component and a device you can justify.

\subsection{Noise source}

A physical process, sampled. The implementation states the process, the
sampling rate, and the claimed \emph{min-entropy per sample}
\[
H_\infty(X) = -\log_2 \max_x \Pr[X = x],
\]
which is the right measure because it bounds the adversary's best single guess
rather than an average over guesses. Raw output is never used directly: it is
biased, correlated, and drifts.

\subsection{Health tests}

Physical sources fail quietly. A laser dims, a bias resistor warms, an
amplifier saturates --- and the output remains bytes of the expected length,
passing casual inspection, while carrying a fraction of the entropy claimed.
Nothing downstream detects this. A key generated from a degraded source
verifies, signs, and behaves correctly in every respect except the one that
matters.

The tests are therefore not diagnostics but part of the source. Two are
required, both from SP 800-90B \S4.4, each sized so that a healthy source trips
it with probability at most $2^{-40}$:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Repetition count}, which catches a source that has stuck;
\item \textbf{Adaptive proportion}, over a window of 512 or 1024 samples, which
catches a source whose distribution has collapsed without stopping.
\end{itemize}

A source failing either stops emitting and reports a fault. It does not degrade
gracefully, because there is no graceful degradation available: half-entropy
output is indistinguishable from full-entropy output at the interface.

\subsection{Conditioning}

Raw samples are compressed by a vetted conditioning function over SHA3-256 or
SHAKE256, fed at least twice the min-entropy it is credited with emitting. This
removes bias and correlation and leaves a uniform string.

\section{Composition}

A deployment has several sources; the operating system's generator is always
one. They combine as
\[
\mathrm{seed} = \mathrm{SHAKE256}\bigl(\text{``LUX-ENTROPY-COMBINE-V1''}
\parallel \mathrm{LE}_{64}(|s_1|) \parallel s_1 \parallel \cdots \parallel
\mathrm{LE}_{64}(|s_n|) \parallel s_n\bigr)
\]
in fixed order, each input length-prefixed, under a tag used nowhere else.

\begin{theorem}[Adding a source cannot weaken the seed]
If any $s_i$ is unpredictable to the adversary, the seed is unpredictable,
regardless of the others.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Argument]
SHAKE256 is preimage-resistant, so predicting the output requires predicting
its input. The length-prefixed encoding is injective, so the input determines
$(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ uniquely; predicting it therefore requires predicting every
$s_i$, including the unpredictable one.
\end{proof}

Two consequences follow, and both are practical rather than decorative. First,
a new and unproven device may be added to a production deployment without risk:
at worst it contributes nothing. Second --- and this is why the encoding is
length-prefixed rather than a plain concatenation --- distinct source sets
cannot collide into one preimage, so two deployments cannot silently derive the
same seed.

The operating system source is mandatory. A deployment whose only source is a
device it cannot itself audit has not eliminated trust; it has relocated it
into a vendor.

\section{Composition with a DRBG}

The entropy source does not serve callers. It seeds a DRBG (SP 800-90A), and
callers consume the DRBG, per the composition of SP 800-90C. In Go the
interface already exists:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Go]
type Source interface {
    io.Reader      // fails closed: an error, never weak bytes
    Health() error // nil while startup and continuous tests pass
}
\end{lstlisting}

Key generation throughout Lux already takes a reader ---
\lstinline{mldsa.GenerateKey(rand.Reader, ...)},
\lstinline{ecdsa.GenerateKey(..., rand.Reader)} --- so adopting a source is
supplying a different reader. There is no consensus change, no fork, and no new
chain. The seam was always there.

\section{Open reference designs}

We publish these because a source whose construction is secret cannot be
assessed, and an entropy source that cannot be assessed is a claim rather than
a component. Each design states what it demonstrates and what it does not.

\subsection{RD-1: avalanche noise (approximately \$15)}

A reverse-biased transistor junction driven into avalanche breakdown, amplified
and sampled by an MCU's ADC. The randomness is carrier-avalanche timing ---
genuinely physical, thermally sensitive, and cheap.

\emph{Demonstrates}: a working entropy source, end to end, with real health
tests and a real min-entropy assessment. This is the design to build first,
because everything difficult about the discipline is present in it.
\emph{Does not demonstrate}: any quantum-optical claim.

\subsection{RD-2: optical shot noise (approximately \$150--300)}

An LED or laser diode, a beamsplitter, and two photodiodes; the sampled
quantity is the difference between the two arms. At low photon flux this
difference is dominated by shot noise, whose origin is the discreteness of
photon arrival.

\emph{Demonstrates}: entropy of quantum-mechanical origin under a stated
physical model. \emph{Does not demonstrate}: that a remote party can verify
this. The device is a better-argued source, not a verifiable one.

The engineering difficulty is not the optics. It is balancing the arms,
stabilising the source against temperature, and showing that the sampled
difference is dominated by shot noise rather than by technical noise --- which
is the entire content of the min-entropy claim.

\subsection{RD-3: vacuum-fluctuation homodyne (laboratory)}

A balanced homodyne detector measuring the vacuum quadrature. The standard
high-rate approach, and the point at which the work becomes a physics project:
optical alignment, local-oscillator stability, and a careful separation of
quantum from technical noise.

\subsection{What none of them establish}

No design here supports a claim of \emph{device-independent} randomness. That
requires a loophole-free Bell test, whose entire purpose is to certify
randomness without trusting the device --- and which is a research programme,
not a board. We name the distinction because it is the one most often elided:
a quantum noise source is trusted hardware, and only a Bell violation is not.

\section{What we do not claim}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Not remotely verifiable.} A recipient of bytes cannot determine
how they were produced. Randomness as a network service is a claim about its
operator.
\item \textbf{Not a consensus input.} See \S\ref{sec:boundary}. Sortition keeps
its deterministic seed.
\item \textbf{Not a standards claim without the assessment.} Conformance to SP
800-90B is a min-entropy claim plus the analysis supporting it. Without the
analysis, citing the standard cites its name.
\item \textbf{Not unbreakable.} The source improves the draw. It says nothing
about the algorithms consuming it, the software around them, or the operator.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

The useful claim is narrower than the marketing available for it, and stronger
for being narrow. Lux's secrets are draws from a source; a well-characterised
physical source with real health tests makes those draws better, composes so
that it cannot make them worse, and plugs into an interface that already
exists. Lux's consensus randomness is a different problem, already solved by
deriving from final state, and hardware entropy would damage it. Holding those
two apart is what makes the hardware safe to sell and safe to run.
