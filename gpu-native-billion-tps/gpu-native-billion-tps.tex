\begin{abstract}
We benchmark, on real silicon, the throughput ceiling of a GPU-native
trade-execution pipeline and ask a precise question: is one billion
trades per second reachable, and if not yet, exactly what stands in the
way? The thesis is that consensus does \emph{not} run per trade --- Lux's
Nova metastable sampling ($O(k\cdot n)$ per node, independent of block
size) finalizes \emph{batch-blocks} that each carry many trades --- so
throughput is set by a five-layer GPU execution pipeline, not by the
consensus round. We measure each layer independently on a four-device
fleet (Apple M1~Max, Apple M4~Max, NVIDIA GB10 Grace-Blackwell, AMD
Radeon~8060S) and then compose.

The results are unambiguous about \emph{where the ceiling is}. The
matching layer is solved: a deterministic, per-book CLOB matcher sustains
a measured \textbf{9.42\,billion} orders/s on one GB10 and
\textbf{12.48\,billion}/s on one Radeon~8060S (\textbf{21.9\,B}/s across
the two discrete GPUs, \textbf{30.3\,B}/s across all four devices), with
GPU-versus-CPU output parity verified bit-for-bit on every run. Matching
is roughly $26{,}000\times$ above the 1\,B/s target. The binding
constraint is elsewhere: Merkle-Patricia \emph{settlement} (state
commit) measures \textbf{160--354\,thousand} fills/s on a ten-core CPU,
and the parallel Block-STM engine that would accelerate it is (verified
empirically) \emph{not wired into the production path} today ---
C-Chain block processing is currently sequential. Full post-quantum
signature verification is a second, honest gap: classical batch-verify
on GPU is fast and real (BLS aggregate $144\times$, ecrecover $32\times$,
BLS12-381 pairings 49{,}197/s on GB10), but the ML-DSA/SLH-DSA GPU
verify kernels are unwired --- the GB10 composite PQ-verify path returns
\texttt{NOT\_SUPPORTED}, so PQ verification is CPU-bound at a measured
${\sim}20{,}000$ verifies/s/core today.

We therefore report the current \emph{proven} end-to-end wired rate
honestly (729 real on-chain settlements on a single Zoo mainnet node;
Lux mainnet not yet re-proven trading post-incident), the measured
per-layer ceilings, and the precise ${\sim}2{,}800\times$ settlement gap
that separates them. One billion trades/s is a settlement-and-wiring
problem, not a matching problem. We give the roadmap that closes it and
the self-heal (``no node left behind'') evidence --- proven green in
simulation this session (rejoin to finalized height in \SI{2.76}{\second},
no fork), not yet on a live multi-box network.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

``One billion trades per second'' is easy to assert and hard to earn.
This paper earns as much of it as the hardware and the code will
currently support, measures the rest, and refuses to paper over the gap.

The architecture rests on one structural decision: \textbf{consensus is
not on the per-trade path}. Lux finalizes with Nova, a metastable
repeated-sampling protocol in the Avalanche family
\cite{avalanche2020,consensusengines}: each validator, each round,
samples $k$ peers and flips its preference on a super-majority; after
$\beta$ consecutive confident rounds the value is final. The per-node
cost is $O(k\cdot n)$ in the sample size, \emph{independent of how many
transactions the block contains}. A block that carries one trade and a
block that carries ten million trades cost the consensus layer the same.
Throughput therefore lives in the \emph{execution} pipeline that fills
those batch-blocks, and that pipeline is what we benchmark.

The pipeline has five layers, each independently GPU-addressable:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{GPU matching} --- a parallel central-limit-order-book
        (CLOB) matcher: price-time priority as a per-book sequential
        walk, millions of books in parallel.
  \item \textbf{GPU Block-STM execution} --- optimistic parallel EVM
        execution with conflict detection and deterministic re-execution.
  \item \textbf{GPU FHE} --- encrypted-state execution kernels
        (NTT-accelerated lattice arithmetic).
  \item \textbf{GPU PQ batch-verify} --- ML-DSA / SLH-DSA / classical
        batch signature verification, the layer that keeps full
        cryptographic security from becoming the bottleneck.
  \item \textbf{Consensus} --- Nova sampling finalizes batch-blocks;
        a behind validator self-heals (``no node left behind'').
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Method and honesty contract.}
Every throughput figure in this paper carries a provenance tag:
\Mnow{} = measured by us this session (2026-07-10) on the fleet of
\S\ref{sec:setup}; \Mprior{} = measured in prior Lux work, cited, not
re-run here; \Proj{} = projected or arithmetically derived; \Claim{} =
external third-party claim. Where a component is a known-answer-test
(KAT)-clean kernel that is not yet wired to the live path, we say so and
measure what \emph{is} runnable. A rigorous paper with honest gaps is
worth more than a fabricated ``1\,B/s achieved.'' No number here is
invented.

\section{The GPU-native execution architecture}\label{sec:arch}

A trade enters as a signed order, is matched against a book, the
resulting fills mutate account and market state, that state is committed
into the world trie, the block is formed, and Nova finalizes it. The
GPU-native design maps each stage to a data-parallel kernel and lets
consensus amortize over a large batch:

\[
\underbrace{\text{ingest}+\text{sig-verify}}_{\text{L4}}
\;\to\;
\underbrace{\text{match}}_{\text{L1}}
\;\to\;
\underbrace{\text{conflict-detect}+\text{execute}}_{\text{L2}}
\;\to\;
\underbrace{\text{settle (state commit)}}_{\text{L2}}
\;\to\;
\underbrace{\text{batch-block}\ \to\ \text{Nova finalize}}_{\text{L5}}
\]

The end-to-end trades/s is the \emph{minimum} stage throughput once
consensus is amortized over the batch. The rest of the paper measures
each $\min$-term. Optional encrypted execution (L3, FHE) sits inside L2
for confidential order flow.

\paragraph{Determinism is the load-bearing invariant.} Every validator
must re-execute a batch-block bit-identically or the chain forks. This
constrains the kernels: the matcher assigns \emph{one GPU thread per
book} and walks that book sequentially (no intra-book parallelism $\to$
no fill reordering), while millions of books run concurrently; the
executor's conflict detector emits the same edge set on GPU and CPU
(KAT-equal); sharded settlement commits the same state root as the
sequential order. We verify each of these below.

\section{Layer 1 --- GPU matching (the billion/s candidate)}\label{sec:l1}

The matcher (\texttt{luxfi/gpu/dex}) is a deterministic per-book CLOB.
Each book has \texttt{DEPTH}=32 resting levels; \texttt{TAKERS}=32 taker
orders cross it in strict price-time priority. One thread owns one book
and performs up to $\texttt{TAKERS}\times\texttt{DEPTH}=1024$ match-steps
--- real compute, not a dispatch microbenchmark. The metric is
\emph{taker-orders matched per second} (ord/s). The inner loop:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C]
for (t = 0; t < TAKERS; t++) {                   // one thread, one book
  tq = t_qty[book,t]; tp = t_price[book,t]; ts = t_side[book,t];
  for (i = 0; i < DEPTH && tq > 0; i++) {        // sequential walk = deterministic
    if (rem[i]==0) continue;
    ok = ts==0 ? (tp>=rp[i]) : (rp[i]>=tp);      // price cross
    if (!ok) continue;
    got = min(tq, rem[i]); tq-=got; rem[i]-=got; // partial fill
  }
}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Findings}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Coalescing is the discrete-GPU win, neutral on Apple.}
        Transposing array-of-structs to order-major struct-of-arrays
        (so a warp reads 32 contiguous words) lifts GB10 from
        \num{1.61} to \SI{9.42}{\text{B\,ord/s}} and the AMD APU from
        \num{0.76} to \SI{12.48}{\text{B\,ord/s}}. On Apple unified
        memory the large shared cache already absorbs the strided
        access, so AoS is the tuned default (M1~Max
        $2.47\!\to\!2.79$; the SoA rewrite is neutral-to-negative).
  \item \textbf{The two discrete backends want opposite tunings.} GB10
        is occupancy-bound $\to$ low register pressure, TPB=32
        ($\to$\,\SI{9.42}{\text{B}}); the AMD APU is bandwidth-rich
        $\to$ register-resident data, TPB=128
        ($\to$\,\SI{12.48}{\text{B}}). No single config wins everywhere.
  \item \textbf{Determinism is free.} Every layout $\times$ occupancy
        $\times$ variant permutation, at every scale $N=1$\,M--16\,M,
        produced identical fills to the OpenMP CPU reference. Tuning
        moves throughput, never the result. This is the L1 half of the
        ``every validator re-executes bit-identically'' invariant.
  \item \textbf{Working-set effects are visible and honest.} At
        $N=16$\,M the GB10 drops to \SI{4.58}{\text{B}} (working set
        exceeds cache $\to$ bandwidth-bound); the peak sits where the
        per-book state stays resident.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Verdict:} a single modern GPU matches at
$9$--$12\times10^{9}$ orders/s. \emph{Matching is not the bottleneck to
$10^9$ trades/s; it clears it by four orders of magnitude.}

\section{Layer 2 --- GPU Block-STM execution and settlement}\label{sec:l2}

Matching produces fills; fills must execute and commit to state. Lux's
parallel executor (\texttt{luxfi/evm/core/parallel}) is Block-STM
\cite{blockstm2022,lp210}: speculatively execute in parallel, detect
read/write conflicts, re-execute conflicts in deterministic index order,
commit. The DEX-fill fast path (\texttt{settlement.go}) applies the same
conflict kernel and shards by market.

\subsection{Determinism: measured green}

The anti-fork property is a test suite, and we re-ran it
(\texttt{CGO\_ENABLED=0}, pure-Go engine). All twelve determinism/parity
cases pass, including the two that matter most:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \texttt{TestConflictEdgesMatchGPUKAT} (5 sub-cases) --- the CPU
        conflict-edge set is \emph{byte-identical} to the GPU
        \texttt{conflict\_detect} kernel's known-answer output.
  \item \texttt{TestSettleShardedEqualsSequential} (shards
        1/3/4/10) --- sharded/parallel settlement commits the
        \emph{same} state root as strict sequential application; a
        hot-market, order-sensitive slot (\texttt{keccak(prev||taker)})
        makes the test non-vacuous.
\end{itemize}
\texttt{ok\ \ github.com/luxfi/evm/core/parallel\ \ 0.896s} --- 12/12
PASS (\Mnow).

Two honest facts fall out of Table~\ref{tab:l2}:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{This Block-STM engine is copy-bound, not
        compute-bound.} It deep-copies the \texttt{StateDB} per
        speculative tx (9.7\,MB, 48k allocs per block), so on light,
        disjoint transactions it \emph{loses to serial}
        ($330$k vs $107$M tx/s) and even on heavy tx it trails serial
        ($47$k vs $287$k). It scales across workers ($1.87\times$ on 10
        cores) but the copy overhead dominates the win. The fix is
        known --- shared multi-version memory (MVHashMap) instead of
        copy-the-world; the sibling \texttt{evmgpu} tree already
        implements \texttt{MvMemory}.
  \item \textbf{Settlement is the on-chain floor.} Sharded settlement
        commits at \SI{354}{k\,fills/s} on 10 cores; the Merkle-Patricia
        state write, not matching, is the binding term. Conflict
        detection (\SI{2.0}{M\,fills/s}) is not binding.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The wiring truth}\label{sec:l2wiring}

We verified empirically (grep of the whole tree; direct build) two
sobering facts that any honest 1\,B/s claim must confront:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{The parallel executor is not on the production path.}
        \texttt{RegisterExecutor(NewBlockSTMExecutor)} is never called;
        \texttt{DefaultExecutor()} resolves to a no-op fallback. Live
        C-Chain block processing is \textbf{100\% sequential today}. The
        Block-STM numbers above are of a real, tested engine that is not
        yet dispatched in production. An independent 2026-07-10 head-to-head
        \cite{benchvsava} confirms this from the other side: the live Lux
        C-Chain and \texttt{avalanchego}'s C-Chain run the \emph{same}
        sequential, geth-derived EVM (identical \texttt{BenchmarkInsertChain}
        suite, identical hardware), so their live throughput is governed by the
        same fee/gas-target throttle --- the Lux advantage at v1.36 is the
        consensus-liveness axis (Nova's 3/5 majority tier), not parallel
        execution, which remains unwired.
  \item \textbf{No arbitrary EVM opcode runs on GPU today.} The GPU
        bridge offloads \emph{ecrecover} only (keccak is a stub); a full
        GPU EVM interpreter is documented future work (``Phase 4''), and
        the \texttt{cevm}/\texttt{revm} backends are honest
        \texttt{return nil,nil} stubs.
\end{itemize}

For context, the GPU-EVM benchmark paper \cite{evmgpubench} reports
(M1~Max, \Mprior) an optimized GPU EVM kernel at \SI{2.3}{Gops/s}
($12.1\times$ a C++ CPU interpreter), end-to-end Block-STM at
$528\!\to\!3390$\,Mgas/s ($6.4\times$ at 10 threads), and GPU ecrecover
at $32\times$ (\SI{1613}{ms}\,$\to\,{\sim}$\SI{50}{ms} for 47k
signatures). Those are measured, but on the \texttt{evmgpu} fork, not on
the wired production executor. The gap between ``kernel exists and is
fast'' and ``kernel is on the live consensus path'' is the central
subject of \S\ref{sec:roadmap}.

\subsection{The PQ cost accounting}

The classical batch-verify story is measured and good: BLS aggregate
$144\times$, ecrecover $32\times$, and on the GB10 the crypto engine runs
at \SI{49197}{pairings/s} while using only $11.9\%$ of streaming-multiprocessor
occupancy \cite{lp203} --- i.e. an order of magnitude of headroom
untouched, at \SI{12300}{pairings/W} versus ${\sim}130$/W on a 20-core
x86 (a $95\times$ energy advantage).

The \textbf{post-quantum} verify story has a precise gap. ML-DSA and
SLH-DSA GPU verify kernels are \emph{not wired}: the GB10 composite
verify wrappers return \texttt{LUX\_BACKEND\_ERROR\_NOT\_SUPPORTED}
(measured status), and the PQ-verify throughput figures in the
GPU-native-verify work are explicitly labelled projected, not measured
\cite{lp203,lp137}. Today, PQ verification is CPU-bound at a measured
${\sim}$\SI{20000}{verif/s/core} for ML-DSA-44 (\SI{50}{\micro s}),
i.e. ${\sim}$\SI{200}{k\,verif/s} across ten cores --- and ML-DSA-65,
the heavier parameter set, costs \SI{134}{\micro s}/core
(${\sim}$\SI{7500}{/s/core}) \cite{evmgpubench}. Relative to classical
ecrecover on the same core, PQ verify is $1.5$--$4\times$ heavier. This
is the true price of ``full post-quantum security'' at the ingress
layer, and it is currently paid on the CPU.

\section{Layer 5 --- Consensus and ``no node left behind''}\label{sec:l5}

\subsection{Batch-block finalization}

The v1.36 consensus is Nova's \textbf{two-tier finality ladder}
\cite{lp305nova}: a block is \emph{locally accepted} at the Nova rung (a simple
majority $\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$, crash-fault-safe) and only \emph{exported} at the
Quasar rung (a strict $>\tfrac{2}{3}$ of stake, Byzantine-safe post-quantum
certificate). The batch-block executor in this paper runs \emph{downstream of Nova
acceptance}; consensus samples a fixed $k$ per round, so its per-node cost is

configuration/DA problem, not a throughput wall.

\subsection{Self-heal: measured green in simulation}\label{sec:selfheal}

The invariant the owner's launch gate rests on: a validator that falls
behind rejoins, and the chain keeps finalizing --- no node left behind.
Two recent fixes were re-run this session:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{node \texttt{v1.34.30}} (size-chunked catch-up):
        \texttt{TestGetContext\_ChunksBySize\_FitsUnderCap} and
        \texttt{\ldots SingleOversizeBlock\_StillServed} both \textbf{PASS}
        --- ``12 blocks, \num{1843344} payload bytes (budget
        \num{1966080}, cap \num{2097152}) --- fits.'' This closes the
        exact bug a 250-trader DEX-load run exposed, where heavy blocks
        overflowed the 2\,MB peer-message cap and stranded a behind
        validator (\Mnow, re-run at the tagged commit).
  \item \textbf{consensus \texttt{v1.35.38}} (intermediate-ancestor
        catch-up): \texttt{TestMultiNode\_PartitionHeal\_Reconverges\-InSeconds}
        \textbf{PASS} --- a 5-node sim ($\alpha{=}4$, $K{=}5$),
        node~4 partitioned before any block, majority advances 35
        $\alpha$-of-$K$-certified finalized blocks, then the engine's
        native \texttt{requestCatchup} reconverges the rejoiner to
        finalized height~35 in \textbf{\SI{2.761}{s}} (bound 15\,s),
        \emph{no fork, no operator restore} (\Mnow, tagged commit).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Honest caveat.} These are \emph{simulation-level} proofs
(Go \texttt{simNet}), not live multi-box/mainnet block-height proofs. On
the current development tree (node \texttt{ship/node-v1.34.24} against
consensus \texttt{ship/consensus-v1.36-nova}) the \texttt{chains}
package does not build --- an in-flight interface refactor
(\texttt{HandleIncomingPrevote} drift) of the v1.36 Nova lock-free
rework --- so the fixes are green only at their tagged commits. Every
mainnet rejoin narrated in operations to date was performed by
\emph{manual} volume-snapshot/PVC-swap because native auto-rejoin was
historically broken; the native fixes above are days old and have no
live-production proof yet. The self-heal property is
\textbf{proven in simulation, pending on a live GPU fleet}.

\section{The current wired baseline (honestly)}\label{sec:baseline}

It is tempting to quote a large end-to-end number. The truth is smaller
and we state it plainly:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{The often-cited ``169'' is a latency, not a rate.} It is
        \SI{169}{ns} average match latency for the single-book C++
        matcher, not 169 fills/s. No ``10{,}039 fills'' figure exists in
        any source we could find. We discard the ``169 fills/s
        baseline'' as a misreading.
  \item \textbf{Best proven end-to-end:} 729 real on-chain
        \texttt{0x9999} settlements on a single Zoo mainnet node (blocks
        808--3599, receipts verified, status \texttt{0x1}) --- a
        single-node, mechanism-proving run, since idle.
  \item \textbf{Lux mainnet:} 0 fills in the last 500 blocks as of
        2026-07-07 --- the DEX was frozen during the C-Chain incident and
        is not yet re-proven trading post-recovery.
\end{itemize}

So the honest gap is not ``169/s $\to 10^9$/s.'' It is: \emph{a proven
single-node settlement mechanism, and a measured 0.35\,M fills/s batched
settlement ceiling, versus a $10^9$/s target} --- with the matching,
determinism, and self-heal pieces independently proven, and the
settlement/wiring/PQ-verify pieces measured and open.

compose the same way.

\section{Threats to validity}

We hold ourselves to the same standard we hold the numbers.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Kernel-only, synthetic books (L1).} The matcher figures
        exclude host-device transfer and use uniform synthetic order
        books. They measure the matching-compute ceiling, not an
        end-to-end fill rate. We label them ord/s precisely for this
        reason and never add them to the end-to-end number.
  \item \textbf{Single-host execution (L2).} Block-STM/settlement is a
        10-core CPU measurement; the GPU state-commit that would move the
        floor is not yet built, so we do not project it as measured.
  \item \textbf{Prior-measured layers (L3, parts of L4/L5).} FHE NTT,
        BLS/pairing/keccak GPU throughput, and consensus finality are
        cited from prior Lux runs (\Mprior), not re-executed here; we
        flag each and do not upgrade a projection to a measurement.
  \item \textbf{Simulation, not live (L5 self-heal).} The reconvergence
        proof is a Go \texttt{simNet}; a live multi-box GPU proof is
        step 8, not done.
  \item \textbf{Composition is a $\min$, not a sum.} The one number we
        emphasize --- the $\sim 2{,}800\times$ settlement gap --- is
        derived from measured stage throughputs; the $30.3$\,B/s fleet
        figure is a sum of independent measured peaks and is a matching
        ceiling, not a trade rate.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

One billion trades per second is reachable on the Lux GPU-native
architecture, and the measurements say precisely why it is not reached
\emph{today}. The matching layer clears the target by four orders of
magnitude --- \SI{9.42}{B\,ord/s} on one GB10, \SI{12.48}{B}/s on one
AMD APU, \SI{30.3}{B}/s across the fleet, every run parity-verified and
deterministic. Consensus is off the per-trade path by construction and
has ample batch-block headroom. Self-heal is green in simulation
(\SI{2.76}{s} rejoin, no fork). What remains is not matching and not
consensus: it is \textbf{settlement} --- a measured \SI{0.35}{M\,fills/s}
Merkle-Patricia commit that must become a GPU-parallel commit
(${\sim}2{,}800\times$) --- plus the \textbf{wiring} of already-fast
kernels onto the live path (Block-STM is tested but unregistered; the
GPU EVM interpreter and the ML-DSA/SLH-DSA verify kernels are unwired),
and a \textbf{live} proof of the self-heal property. Those are the exact,
honest gaps. The billion is a settlement-and-wiring problem, and this
paper measures its size to the order of magnitude.
