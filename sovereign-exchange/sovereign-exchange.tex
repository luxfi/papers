\begin{abstract}
We describe the \emph{Lux Institutional Exchange}: a white-label venue on
which an operator runs a fully regulated market --- Alternative Trading
System (ATS), Broker-Dealer (BD), and Transfer Agent (TA) --- whose
trust-critical functions are \emph{native protocol primitives of a sovereign
Lux L1} rather than bolted-on vendors. The design targets institutional
high-frequency trading and settles it with cryptographic finality. Its
central properties are four. (i) \textbf{HFT-grade matching}: a deterministic
central-limit-order-book engine matches in \textbf{169\,ns} on average
(p50~125\,ns, p99~292\,ns) at \textbf{5.91M orders/s} single-threaded and
\textbf{11.88M orders/s} across ten threads, with 49\,ns cancels and
bit-exact CPU/GPU parity. (ii) \textbf{Non-custodial custody}: the venue
never takes possession of client assets --- balances sit under a dealerless
threshold-MPC key that is never assembled at any single party, so the ATS is
non-custodial by construction and carries none of the qualified-custodian
single-point-of-compromise risk. (iii) \textbf{Real-time settlement}: fills
settle atomically \emph{inside the block}, delivery-versus-payment, at T+0 ---
the chain is the tape and the register. (iv) \textbf{Global regulatory
reach}: jurisdiction is a declarative configuration axis, so the same core
instantiates a U.S., Canadian, EU (MiCA), U.K., or Singapore venue by
swapping a brand profile, with FHE-confidential compliance keeping client PII
off-chain. We report the on-chain settlement primitive running live on Lux
mainnet, and give the security and performance properties that make the
architecture suitable for regulated, institutional trading of both crypto and
tokenized securities.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

An institutional trading venue is, structurally, four obligations bound
together: it must \emph{onboard} counterparties under KYC/AML rules (the
broker-dealer function), \emph{match} their orders fairly and at competitive
speed and record the trades (the ATS function), \emph{settle} and keep the
authoritative ownership register (the transfer-agent function), and
\emph{custody} the assets throughout. Incumbents discharge each obligation
with a separate vendor --- a KYC provider, a matching engine, a
books-and-records database, a qualified custodian --- and the operator
inherits the union of their risks. Two of those risks dominate. The
\emph{custodian} is a single institution (or HSM boundary) that, if
compromised or coerced, moves client assets unilaterally. And the
\emph{matching-plus-settlement} split forces a choice every venue makes
badly: fast centralized matching with slow, trust-based settlement, or slow
on-chain matching with no real throughput.

The Lux Institutional Exchange refuses both trade-offs. Matching runs at
hardware speed in a deterministic order-book engine; settlement runs on-chain
with cryptographic finality; and custody is \emph{non-custodial} --- assets
never enter an exchange omnibus account, they move under a dealerless
threshold key. The venue is delivered white-label: an operator supplies a
declarative brand-and-jurisdiction profile and provisions a tenant. This
paper specifies the architecture, grounds each claim in the shipped
implementation and measured benchmarks, and states the properties that follow.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item An HFT-grade matching layer (\S\ref{sec:hft}) with published
        nanosecond-class latencies and multi-million-order-per-second
        throughput, with deterministic CPU/GPU parity.
  \item A \emph{non-custodial} ATS custody model (\S\ref{sec:custody}) in
        which no party ever holds the signing key.
  \item Real-time, in-block, delivery-versus-payment settlement
        (\S\ref{sec:settle}), reported running live on Lux mainnet.
  \item A securities framework (\S\ref{sec:securities}) spanning crypto,
        tokenized public equities, and private offerings.
  \item Global regulatory reach as configuration (\S\ref{sec:global}), with
        FHE-confidential compliance (\S\ref{sec:fhe}).
\end{itemize}

The system has three tiers (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}): a \emph{configuration}
surface (SuperAdmin + tenant platform) that provisions and brands tenants and
binds external providers; an \emph{operator} surface (ATS/BD/TA) exposing the
regulated functions as role-scoped consoles; and a \emph{protocol substrate}

on-chain settlement, bridging, and FHE confidentiality --- running on a
sovereign L1 per tenant, isolated at consensus (the tenant's primary-network
identifier equals its EVM chain identifier).

\section{HFT-Grade Matching}
\label{sec:hft}

The order-matching core is a deterministic central-limit-order-book (CLOB)
engine. It is not a smart contract loop; it is a purpose-built matcher with a
C++ hot path and a portable Go reference, benchmarked under
\texttt{cmd/dexbench}.

\centering

\begin{tabular}{@{}lllr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Engine} & \textbf{Avg match} & \textbf{p50 / p99} & \textbf{Throughput}\\
\midrule
C++ (single thread) & 169\,ns & 125\,ns / 292\,ns & 5.91M orders/s\\
C++ (10 threads)    & ---     & ---               & 11.88M orders/s\\
Pure Go (\texttt{pkg/lx}) & 381\,ns & --- & 2.2M orders/s\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Measured matching performance (\texttt{luxfi/dex}). Order-cancel on

\label{tab:hft}
\end{table}

\begin{property}[Nanosecond, deterministic matching]
The engine matches at a mean of 169\,ns with a 292\,ns 99th percentile and
sustains millions of orders per second single-threaded. Determinism (per-book,
CPU/GPU-identical) means the match sequence is reproducible and auditable ---
a requirement for a regulated ATS that a nondeterministic or
approximate-accelerated engine cannot meet.
\end{property}

Two architectural choices make this compatible with an on-chain venue rather
than at odds with it. First, the \textbf{wire path is zero-copy}: the node's
ZAP transport removes serialization and HTTP/2 overhead on the hot path (it is
several times faster to (de)serialize than protobuf and materially lower
latency), so order ingress and market-data egress are not the bottleneck the
matcher is fast enough to expose. Second, matching is \emph{separated from
settlement}: the engine produces fills at hardware speed, and those fills
settle on-chain (\S\ref{sec:settle}) with cryptographic finality. The venue
thus gets HFT latency \emph{and} trustless settlement, instead of choosing
one.

\section{Non-Custodial Custody}
\label{sec:custody}

Custody is where incumbent venues are weakest and where regulators scrutinize
hardest: a qualified custodian, or the HSM inside it, is one entity that can
move assets. The Lux venue removes that entity. Client assets are never held
in an exchange account; they sit under a \emph{dealerless threshold-MPC} key
on the M-Chain.

\begin{definition}[Dealerless threshold custody]
A group of $n$ validators holds a $t$-of-$n$ threshold signing capability such
that (i) the private key is never computed or stored at any single party at
any time, including at generation; (ii) any $t$ parties jointly sign; and
(iii) any coalition of fewer than $t$ learns nothing about the key.
\end{definition}

Keys are generated by a verifiable secret-sharing DKG --- each party samples
its own polynomial and publishes commitments; the group key is the sum of
contributions, so no dealer ever holds it. Signing is threshold ECDSA
(CGGMP21-style) or Schnorr (FROST); the output is byte-identical to an
ordinary signature and verifies under the standard verifier, so the scheme is
transparent to every wallet and chain.

\begin{property}[The ATS is non-custodial]
No single validator, no operator, and no platform ever holds material
sufficient to move client assets. The exchange does not take custody; it
coordinates a threshold that the client's own key share participates in.
Asset movement requires a $t$-of-$n$ threshold, so there is no
qualified-custodian single point of compromise and no exchange omnibus honeypot.
\end{property}

For a regulated venue this is decisive: non-custodial operation reduces the
operator's custody liability and capital burden, and converts the strongest
counterparty risk in the industry --- ``the exchange holds my assets'' --- into
a cryptographic threshold the client participates in.

\section{Real-Time On-Chain Settlement}
\label{sec:settle}

Fills settle on a native settlement precompile at the reserved address
\texttt{0x9999}. An order and its fill are ordinary transactions; the fill
settles \emph{atomically inside the block that includes it}, moving both legs
--- delivery of the asset and payment --- in a single receipt. Settlement is
therefore \textbf{real-time (T+0), delivery-versus-payment, and final on
inclusion}: there is no T+1/T+2 window, no settlement-risk exposure between
match and finality, and no separate clearing intermediary. The chain is both
the trade tape and the ownership register, so the venue is self-auditing.

\paragraph{Live evidence.}
The settlement primitive is running on Lux mainnet. A maker bid and a taker
fill executed against a live concentrated-liquidity pool: the taker sold 10
units of the base asset into a bid priced at 1.1, and the settlement receipt
carried the on-chain fill event with output amount 11 together with both
ERC-20 transfer legs in the same accepted transaction, conserving value
exactly ($10 \times 1.1 = 11$). Real-time, atomic, on-chain settlement is a
shipped, exercised primitive --- not a specification.

\section{The Securities Framework}
\label{sec:securities}

One venue, one order book, one custody root, one register --- across three
asset classes:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Crypto} --- spot markets (e.g.\ BTC, ETH, and native assets)
        under the same non-custodial custody and real-time settlement.
  \item \textbf{Tokenized public equities} --- shares represented on-chain
        (identified by their real CUSIPs), traded on the ATS and registered by
        the TA, with corporate actions (dividends, splits) as native
        operations.
  \item \textbf{Private offerings} --- Reg~D~506(c) accredited raises, Reg~A+
        tiers, and Reg~CF crowdfunding, issued through the TA and gated by
        FHE-evaluated accreditation checks, with an on-chain cap table.
\end{itemize}

Because the ATS/BD/TA roles are the same regardless of asset class, a single
venue lists a crypto pair, a tokenized equity, and a private placement side by
side --- something no incumbent stack does without three separate systems.

\section{Global Regulatory Reach}
\label{sec:global}

Each tenant runs its own sovereign primary network (network ID $=$ EVM chain
ID), isolated at consensus. A declarative brand profile then specializes it,
and \emph{jurisdiction is one of the axes}: the same substrate and the same
operator services instantiate venues in different regulatory regimes by
swapping providers and compliance parameters --- not by rewriting code.

\begin{remark}
The custody, matching, settlement, and confidentiality primitives are
jurisdiction-invariant. Expanding to a new regime is a provider-binding and
compliance-rules exercise, not a re-architecture --- which is what makes a
single white-label core viable as a \emph{global} exchange platform.
\end{remark}

\section{FHE-Confidential Compliance}
\label{sec:fhe}

A chain-authoritative venue must not publish the client data that securities
and privacy law protect. Sensitive state --- beneficial ownership on the cap
table, accreditation and sanctions attributes, pre-print order size --- is
stored as ciphertext, and the compliance predicates that gate activity
(accreditation thresholds, sanctions/PEP screening, position and concentration
limits) are evaluated \emph{over the ciphertext} with lattice-based FHE, with
threshold decryption gated by the MPC group.

\begin{property}[Authoritative without disclosure]
For a compliance predicate $P$ and encrypted client attributes
$\mathsf{Enc}(x)$, the venue computes $\mathsf{Enc}(P(x))$ and reveals only the
boolean outcome under threshold decryption; $x$ is never disclosed to
validators, the operator, or counterparties.
\end{property}

\section{Cross-Chain Bridging}
\label{sec:bridge}

Assets originating on other chains enter through the B-Chain bridge, attested
by the \emph{same} dealerless-MPC group that provides custody. A transfer is
authorized by a threshold signature of the validator group; no bridge operator
holds a unilateral minting key, and bridge fees are taken from the bridged
amount. Because bridging shares the custody root, a bridged asset is held under
the same non-custodial threshold guarantee as a natively issued one --- there
is no weaker bridge-custody tier.

\section{Security and Performance Properties}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{HFT matching.} 169\,ns average match (p99 292\,ns), up to
        11.88M orders/s, deterministic with CPU/GPU parity (\S\ref{sec:hft}).
  \item \textbf{Non-custodial.} No party holds the signing key; movement
        needs a $t$-of-$n$ threshold; no omnibus honeypot (\S\ref{sec:custody}).
  \item \textbf{Real-time settlement.} Atomic, in-block, DvP, T+0; finality on
        inclusion; live on mainnet (\S\ref{sec:settle}).
  \item \textbf{Multi-asset.} Crypto, tokenized equities, and private
        offerings on one book and register (\S\ref{sec:securities}).
  \item \textbf{Global.} Jurisdiction as configuration; one audited core, many
        regimes (\S\ref{sec:global}).
  \item \textbf{Confidential.} Compliance over ciphertext; client PII never
        on-chain in clear (\S\ref{sec:fhe}).
  \item \textbf{Tenant isolation.} Each tenant a sovereign L1 isolated at
        consensus.
  \item \textbf{Post-quantum root.} Lattice-based FHE and the Lux
        post-quantum certificate/threshold constructions underpin
        confidentiality, finality, and custody.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work and Conclusion}

The design continues the ``chain-first'' line --- the blockchain as
authoritative source of truth for regulated financial infrastructure --- and
extends it from a settlement-and-registry substrate to a complete,
institutional-grade venue. Its distinguishing choices are (i) an HFT matching
core that makes on-chain settlement compatible with professional trading
rather than a compromise against it; (ii) \emph{non-custodial} custody, which
removes the qualified-custodian single point of compromise instead of
hardening it; (iii) real-time DvP settlement with cryptographic finality; and
(iv) jurisdiction-as-configuration, which makes one audited core a global
platform. We reported the settlement primitive running live on Lux mainnet and
the matching benchmarks from the shipped engine. The result is a white-label
substrate on which a regulated, high-frequency venue for crypto and tokenized
securities --- in any supported jurisdiction --- is a configuration over a
shared, cryptographically-grounded core.
